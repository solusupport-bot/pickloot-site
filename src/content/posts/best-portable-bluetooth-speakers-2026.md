Portable speakers have converged on one feature list: everything claims waterproofing, long battery life and app control. What manufacturers actually publish is narrower - a rated output power, a battery figure with or without its test conditions, an IP rating, a Bluetooth version and a weight. This guide covers what each of those changes, and where it stops telling you anything.

## Rated output power is a ceiling, not a quality score

Watts describe the electrical power the amplifier delivers, not how good the result sounds. A 5 W speaker and a 35 W speaker are not separated by a factor of seven in perceived loudness - roughly a tenfold increase in power is needed to sound about twice as loud - so the practical gap is closer to "fills a bathroom" versus "fills a patio."

Where the number matters is headroom. A speaker running near its ceiling distorts; one running well below it does not. Output power predicts behaviour at high volume, not how it sounds at conversational volume - which is where most listening happens.

Only one of the three options here publishes a rated output power at all, and it splits the figure across a woofer and a tweeter. A two-driver design divides the frequency range between a driver built for bass and one for treble - a structural difference from a single full-range driver, visible in the spec sheet even where the wattage is not comparable across brands.

## Battery figures are only comparable when the test volume is published

Every manufacturer states a maximum. Very few state the conditions. A "20 hours" figure measured at 40% volume in a neutral EQ mode and a "24 hours" figure with no stated conditions are not the same kind of number, and neither survives loud outdoor listening - volume is the dominant drain, and bass-heavy EQ profiles shorten runtime further.

Read the published figure as capacity relative to draw, then expect real use to land below it. The more comparable spec, where it exists, is fast charging: a stated "ten minutes gives two hours" is something you can plan an evening around, in a way a headline maximum is not.

## IP ratings describe two separate failures

The two digits are independent. The first is solid ingress, mostly dust; the second is water. IP67 means dust-tight and rated for immersion in one metre of water for thirty minutes. IP68 keeps the dust rating and extends the water test beyond one metre, to a depth the manufacturer specifies.

For a speaker the practical difference is small - both survive rain, a pool deck and a spilled drink. What neither covers is the failure people actually meet: drops onto hard ground. That is a separate published test, and only one option here states one, at one metre. Floating is a separate claim again, a function of enclosure density rather than the IP rating.

## Bluetooth version and codecs set the connection, not the sound quality

Bluetooth 5.4 versus an older version mainly affects connection stability, range and power draw. It does not improve audio quality on its own. The codec does more: SBC is the universal baseline, AAC is what Apple devices use, and both are lossy.

That is why a wired path matters on the spec sheet. One option here publishes high-resolution lossless audio over USB-C - a claim about a cable rather than about Bluetooth, and only for compatible source material. Where a listing quotes a Bluetooth class instead of a version number, that describes transmit power and is not a substitute for the version.

## Matching the specs to where you'll use it

Rather than one answer, the useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **It is going near water** - the IP rating covers immersion, but a published floating claim is what matters if it can go over the side of a dock.
- **You listen outdoors at volume** - rated output power and driver count, because that is where distortion appears.
- **You are away from power for days** - the published battery maximum, plus any stated fast-charge figure.
- **You carry it in a pocket or on a strap** - published weight, which varies by more than a factor of two here.
- **You want a wired path around lossy compression** - look for a lossless-over-USB-C line, not a Bluetooth version.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I connect two of the same speaker for stereo sound?**

Pairing two units of the same model into a stereo pair is common in this category, and both the soundcore and JBL options publish multi-speaker pairing features. Mixing different models or brands generally is not supported, so check the manufacturer's wording before buying a second unit for it.

**How waterproof is "waterproof" — can these be submerged?**

IP67 is defined as immersion in one metre of fresh water for thirty minutes; IP68 extends that to a greater depth set by the manufacturer. Both are short, still-water tests, not ratings for swimming with the speaker or for pressurised jets, and neither covers salt water or chlorine over time.

**Do these work as a hands-free speakerphone for calls?**

Speakers in this class include a microphone, but manufacturers rarely publish a microphone specification - a reasonable signal about how central the feature is. Treat call support as a convenience rather than something you can compare between models.
