Air quality monitors look like a single product category until you compare what they actually measure -- some units track one pollutant with heavily documented lab-grade precision, others track seven with far less detail published about accuracy. Four specs explain most of the real difference: which pollutants a unit covers, what sensor technology does the measuring, how you read the results, and how it's powered. This guide walks through each one, then compares three monitors that take meaningfully different approaches to the same basic job.

## Pollutants measured is the first fork in the road

Some monitors are single-purpose instruments: one sensor, one number, documented in detail. Others bundle five to seven pollutants -- particulates, CO2, VOCs, sometimes radon -- onto one device and one screen. Broader coverage isn't automatically better; it usually means less documentation per sensor and a device built around convenience rather than any single reading's precision. Radon in particular is worth flagging on its own: it's the one pollutant here that only one of these three monitors measures at all, and it's not something the other two are quietly estimating -- they simply don't track it.

## Sensor type determines how much you can trust a single number

The most useful spec on a monitor's page isn't the pollutant list, it's the sensor technology and its stated accuracy range, when the manufacturer publishes one. An NDIR (non-dispersive infrared) CO2 sensor with a published accuracy of plus or minus a fixed ppm value and a percentage of the reading is a spec you can actually evaluate and compare between products. A manufacturer that lists "CO2 sensing" with no sensor model or accuracy figure is asking you to trust the number without the means to check it. This isn't just a budget-vs-premium split, either -- in this category, the most fully-specified sensor info came from the single-pollutant device, not the one with the most sensors.

## Display format changes whether you need a phone to read it

E-ink displays trade color and detail for low power draw and a screen you can read in direct sunlight without a glare problem -- a meaningful reason a device can run for years on a couple of AA batteries instead of needing a daily charge. Color touchscreens pack more simultaneous readings into one glance and make historical trends easier to page through, at the cost of real power draw, which is exactly where the rechargeable-battery runtimes in this category get short. A status-glow indicator (a colored ring or light, separate from the main display) adds a readable-from-across-the-room signal that neither pure e-ink nor a touchscreen gives you on their own.

## Power source sets how much maintenance the monitor needs

Replaceable AA batteries are the lowest-maintenance option by far when the rated life is measured in years rather than hours, since you're not tracking a charge cycle at all. Built-in rechargeable batteries push runtime down into single-digit hours on some units, which in practice means leaving the monitor on USB power most of the time rather than treating it as cordless. One monitor in this category does double duty when plugged in, acting as a WiFi relay for other sensors from the same brand elsewhere in the home -- worth knowing if you're planning to add more than one unit later, since that can change which model you want to plug in permanently versus run on battery.
