Mechanical keyboards mostly look the same once you strip the RGB marketing away, but four published specs account for nearly all the practical difference: the switch, how it connects, whether the switch sockets are hot-swappable, and what rollover it supports. None of these show up in a glance at the keycaps. This guide walks through what each spec actually changes, then lays three models side by side so you can match the numbers to how you actually type.

## Switch type decides feel, not build quality

"Mechanical" only tells you there's a physical switch under each key — it says nothing about how that switch feels. Blue switches (like the Outemu Blue here) click and have a tactile bump, which is popular for typing but audible enough to bother anyone nearby. Brown switches are tactile without the click, a middle ground. Red switches are linear — smooth from top to bottom, no bump, generally preferred for fast repeated presses.

Manufacturers publish the switch brand and color, but "feel" beyond that is subjective — actuation force and travel distance vary switch to switch even within the same color name, and not every maker publishes those numbers. Where a spec sheet gives you the switch family, that's the fact; the rest is preference.

## Connectivity sets where the keyboard can live, and what it costs you

A wired-only keyboard has one job and does it without the compromises wireless introduces. Once Bluetooth is in the picture, two published numbers matter more than the "wireless" label itself: how many devices it can stay paired to, and what happens to the polling rate when you disconnect the cable. Both Bluetooth models here support up to three paired devices and both drop from 1000 Hz wired to 90 Hz wireless — a fact worth knowing before assuming "wireless" and "wired" behave identically.

For most typing use, 90 Hz is imperceptible. For competitive gaming input timing, it isn't, which is why wired mode still exists on wireless boards rather than being phased out.

## Hot-swappable sockets change what "customizing" means

A hot-swap socket lets you pull a switch out and push in a different one without a soldering iron — the keyboard's plastic or PCB doesn't care which switch is seated, within its supported pin count. The published difference between these three is in how wide that support goes: the budget board is hot-swappable only within its own switch's footprint, the mid-range hot-swap version is built around one switch family, and the premium board publishes compatibility with both 3-pin and 5-pin MX-style switches, which covers most switches sold separately.

If you never plan to change a switch, this spec is close to irrelevant. If you're the kind of person who might want a different switch in six months, it's the one to check before buying, since a non-hot-swap board makes that decision permanent.

## Key rollover is a floor, not a differentiator, until it isn't

All three keyboards here publish full N-key rollover (NKRO), meaning every key you press registers regardless of how many others are already held down. That used to separate budget keyboards from the rest; today it's table stakes across this category; the more useful thing to check is whether NKRO is published as working over the *wireless* connection too, since Bluetooth HID has historically limited simultaneous keypresses on some hardware. Both wireless boards here publish full NKRO in both modes — a spec worth confirming rather than assuming on any wireless board you're considering.

## Matching the specs to your desk

- **The keyboard never leaves one desk** — connectivity barely matters; optimize for switch type and hot-swap range instead.
- **You alternate between a laptop and a desktop** — Bluetooth multi-device pairing is the spec to check, along with what the polling rate does on disconnect.
- **You expect to swap switches later** — hot-swap pin compatibility (3-pin vs. a single switch family) is the spec that determines your options down the line.
- **Fast, repeated keypresses matter (gaming, typing tests)** — polling rate in wired mode is the ceiling; wireless mode is a different, lower number on the same board.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher price always mean a better switch?**

No — price tracks the feature mix (case material, connectivity, hot-swap breadth, firmware support), not necessarily switch quality. A budget board's switch can feel perfectly fine for typing; the premium tier is paying mostly for wireless flexibility and wider hot-swap compatibility, not a categorically superior switch.

**Can I mix switch brands on a hot-swappable board?**

Generally yes, as long as the pin count matches what the board publishes — a 5-pin-compatible socket accepts most 3-pin and 5-pin MX-style switches regardless of brand. A board that only lists compatibility with one switch family is narrower, and mixing outside that range isn't guaranteed to seat correctly.

**Why does the wireless polling rate matter if I'm not gaming?**

For everyday typing it doesn't — 90 Hz is well above what a human typing cadence needs. It only becomes a practical concern for inputs that depend on fine timing, like fast gaming or certain music/rhythm applications, which is why manufacturers publish both the wired and wireless numbers separately rather than one blended figure.
