Office chairs photograph almost identically: a mesh back, a five-star base, a row of levers under the seat. "Ergonomic" appears on nearly all of them and is not a regulated term, so it tells you nothing. What separates one chair from another is a short list of published adjustment figures - how far the seat slides, how far the backrest reclines, how many axes the armrests travel on, and what load the frame is rated to carry.

## Seat depth adjustment decides whether the chair fits your legs

This is the spec most often missing at the cheaper end, and the one that changes most for people at either end of the height range. Seat depth is the distance from the backrest to the front edge of the seat. Too deep and that edge presses into the back of your knees; too shallow and your thighs are unsupported.

The published figure to look for is the range of travel, not the seat dimension. Two inches of slide covers most of the adult range around a fixed midpoint. A chair publishing only a fixed depth is set at a size suiting the middle of the distribution. If you are notably tall or short, this is the first spec to check and the hardest to work around later.

## Recline range matters at the top of the arc, not the middle

Every chair in this category reclines through roughly 105° to 120°, which covers normal desk posture. The specs only diverge above that. A backrest stopping at 120° holds you close to upright; one reaching 132° lets you tip back far enough that your weight shifts off the seat and onto the back - a reading or call position.

The second half of this spec is the tilt lock. A published list of lock positions - four discrete angles, say - means you can fix the backrest at a chosen stage; free-float recline moves with you instead. If only a maximum angle is published, the lock behaviour is unknown before you sit in it.

## Armrest axes: what 3D and 4D actually move

The number before the D is the count of directions the armrest travels in. Three axes is normally height, sliding forward and back, and lateral movement in or out. A fourth adds pivot, so the pad can angle inward toward the keyboard or outward.

What this changes physically is where your forearm lands. If the armrest cannot come in close enough, your elbows sit wider than your shoulders and the load moves to your upper back; if it cannot drop far enough, your shoulders ride up. Height is the axis that matters most, and every chair here has it. The pivot on a 4D armrest matters mainly if you type with your hands angled inward.

## Headrest travel and load rating

A headrest only does something if it meets the back of your skull rather than your neck, so the published travel figure matters more than its presence. Three to four inches of lift plus a stated tilt or rotation angle covers most seated heights. A mid-back chair publishes no headrest figure - a design choice, not a missing spec.

Weight capacity is the frame and gas-lift rating the maker publishes, usually 300 or 330 lbs. It is a durability figure rather than a comfort one: sustained loading near the limit shortens a gas cylinder's life. An unpublished rating means that axis cannot be compared.

## Matching the specs to your desk

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest in your setup.

- **You are notably taller or shorter than average** - seat depth travel, because nothing else compensates for a seat the wrong length for your thigh.
- **The chair has to slide under a shelf or sloped ceiling** - a mid-back design without a headrest; overall height, not comfort, is the limit.
- **You lean back for calls and reading** - the top of the recline range and the presence of tilt lock positions.
- **You are near the published load ratings** - check a rating is published at all, since not every maker states one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do adjustable chairs like these actually help with back discomfort?**

Genuine adjustability - seat depth, lumbar position, armrest height - is what lets a chair match your body rather than the average of everyone's, and that fit is what the published specs describe. A chair on its own will not resolve an ongoing back problem; treat the fit as one part of a larger change.

**How long do office chairs in this range typically last?**

The makers here publish three-year warranties covering material and workmanship defects, which is the only durability figure any of them state. Warranty length is not a life expectancy, but it marks how long the maker will stand behind the mechanism. Squeaking, sinking, or a seat that no longer holds its height are the usual signs the gas lift is going.

**Is seat depth adjustment necessary, or is it a nice-to-have?**

It depends on how close your leg length sits to the middle of the range a fixed seat is built around. Near the middle, you will not notice it missing. Away from it, the seat edge digs into the back of your knees or leaves your thighs unsupported, and no other adjustment corrects for that.
