Wireless earbuds for studying and commuting all describe themselves the same way: noise cancelling, long battery, clear calls. The published spec sheets separate them more usefully than the marketing does. Four figures account for most of the difference you would notice across a school day — driver size, the noise-cancelling implementation, rated playtime, and codec support. This guide explains what each one changes, then lays three models out side by side.

## Driver size does not track tier

The driver is the diaphragm that moves air, and its diameter is published in millimetres. It is tempting to read a bigger number as a bigger sound, and the three models here show why that fails: the budget pair publishes an 11 mm driver, the premium pair 8.4 mm, the mid-range one just 5 mm. Diameter sets how much air the earbud *can* move, which mostly bears on low-frequency output. It says nothing about how the maker chose to tune it.

Past roughly 6 mm, how well the tip seals your ear canal matters more for perceived bass than another couple of millimetres of driver. Treat the figure as a design note rather than a ranking.

## Noise cancelling is published as a feature, not a strength

Every model here states that it cancels noise, and each names its modes — "Adaptive ANC" on one, "Ambient Sound" and "Quick Attention" on the two Sony pairs. What none of them publishes is an attenuation figure in decibels. That absence is the important part: you cannot compare cancelling *strength* across brands from spec sheets, only whether the feature and its pass-through modes exist.

What ANC does reliably is flatten steady low-frequency sound — ventilation hum, a bus engine. It does much less against speech, because voices are irregular and sit higher in the frequency range. If your problem is a talkative study space rather than a droning one, this is the spec least likely to solve it.

## Rated playtime is quoted under conditions, and the conditions differ

This is where spec sheets stop being directly comparable. Sony publishes two numbers per model, with cancelling on and off: 8.5 and 12 hours for the mid-range pair, 8 and 12 for the premium one. The budget pair publishes 12 hours for the earbuds and 60 hours including the charging case, without stating whether cancelling was on.

Two things follow. First, the premium model's cancelling-on figure is slightly lower than the cheaper Sony's, so playtime does not climb with tier. Second, an earbud-only figure and a with-case figure answer different questions — how long a session can run, versus how long you can go between finding a cable. Sony does not publish a with-case total on these pages at all, so that column cannot be compared across all three.

## Codec support depends on what your phone sends

A codec is the compression scheme the phone uses to send audio over Bluetooth. SBC is the universal floor and AAC is near-universal; both Sony pairs publish those. The premium pair adds LDAC and LC3, which carry more data. The budget pair does not publish its codec list.

This spec only pays off if both ends support it. An iPhone sends AAC and does not support LDAC, so on that device the premium codec list changes nothing. On an Android phone with LDAC enabled, it is a real difference — check your handset before treating this column as a reason to move up a tier.

## Matching the specs to your study day

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest.

- **You study where the noise is steady** — ventilation, traffic, a busy café — cancelling is worth having, and the mode names tell you whether there is a pass-through for hearing announcements.
- **You study where the noise is people talking** — cancelling helps least here; prioritise seal and fit over the ANC column.
- **You forget to charge** — the with-case figure, where the manufacturer publishes one.
- **You listen on an iPhone** — the codec column is largely moot; weigh the other four.
- **They live in a gym bag** — water resistance, where IPX5 adds spray resistance over IPX4's splash rating.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need noise cancelling for lecture halls specifically?**

It depends on what the room sounds like. Cancelling is most effective against the constant low hum of ventilation and projectors, which large halls often have plenty of. It does much less against a neighbour whispering, so if that is the actual problem, a well-sealing tip will do more than the ANC spec.

**Why does the mid-range pair publish a longer cancelling-on battery life than the premium one?**

Because playtime follows how much power the electronics draw and how large the cell is, not the tier. The premium model spends power on extra codecs and processing. Published figures are also measured by each maker under its own conditions, which is why cross-brand battery comparisons need reading carefully.

**Are cheaper earbuds cutting corners on the specs or somewhere else?**

Often somewhere else. The budget pair here publishes the largest driver and the highest water-resistance rating of the three; what it omits is a codec list and a cancelling-on battery figure. The gap at lower tiers tends to show up as unpublished specs rather than worse headline numbers.
