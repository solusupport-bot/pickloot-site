Burr grinders all do the same basic job — crush beans between two burrs instead of chopping them with blades — but the burr type, how many grind settings you get, how you adjust between them, and which brew methods a grinder is actually built for vary a lot between models that look similar on a shelf.

## Conical vs. flat burrs changes the flavor profile, not just the price

Conical burrs are the more common choice for home grinders built around drip, pour-over, and French press — they run quieter, stay cooler during long grinding sessions, and are generally forgiving across brew methods. Flat burrs are associated with sharper flavor separation and are the more common choice on grinders marketed specifically for espresso. Neither type is strictly better; the right choice depends on which brew methods you actually use most, not on price alone, since flat-burr grinders show up at multiple price points.

## Grind setting range matters less than how evenly spaced the steps are

A higher number of settings sounds like more control, but published setting counts aren't directly comparable across brands — one grinder's "40 settings" and another's "60 settings" don't necessarily cover the same practical range from coarse French press to fine espresso. What matters more in practice is whether the steps near the fine end (where espresso lives) are close enough together to dial in precisely, something spec sheets rarely quantify on their own.

## Stepped vs. stepless adjustment is a real usability difference

Stepped grinders click between fixed positions, which makes repeating a grind size trivial — useful if you brew the same thing daily and don't want to hunt for a dial position. Stepless grinders adjust continuously, which is preferred by people chasing a precise espresso grind but makes it harder to return to an exact previous setting without marking it yourself. None of the three grinders compared here are stepless; if infinite adjustment specifically matters to you, that's worth searching for as its own spec.

## Brew-method range: check what the grinder is actually marketed for

Not every burr grinder is built with espresso in mind, even though all of them can technically grind fine. Grinders positioned for drip, pour-over, and French press are tuned and marketed around that coarser-to-medium range, while espresso-capable grinders are built and marketed to also hit a consistent fine grind. Buying a drip-focused grinder expecting espresso-level consistency — or the reverse, overpaying for espresso capability you'll never use — is the most common mismatch in this category.

## Picking between these three in practice

If you mainly brew drip or pour-over and don't care about espresso, the entry-level conical grinder covers the job without paying for capability you won't use. If you want a grinder with a long track record among home baristas and don't need espresso either, the mid-tier conical option is the safer long-term pick over a cheaper no-name alternative. If espresso is part of your routine alongside drip or French press, the flat-burr option built with espresso in mind is worth the extra cost — just don't expect its published settings count to be directly comparable to the stepped conical grinders above it, since the numbers aren't measured the same way across brands.
