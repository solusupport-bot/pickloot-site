Cold plunge tubs mostly look like a barrel or a stock tank with a lid, and the marketing photos don't tell you much. What actually separates them are five published specs: how much water they hold, what temperature range they can reach, whether a chiller is included, how thick the insulation is, and what electrical circuit they need. This guide explains what each one changes, then lines up three models - unchilled, mid-tier chiller, and heat-and-cool - so you can match the numbers to your own space.

## Water capacity tells you how much the chiller (or the ice) has to work with

More water means more thermal mass: the temperature drifts more slowly once you get in and once a chiller starts working. A 105-gallon tank holds its temperature through a longer session than a smaller one, but it also takes longer to bring down from tap temperature - whether that's ice melting into it or a chiller running continuously.

Not every manufacturer publishes a single capacity figure. Some list an "operating" range depending on fill level versus total tank volume, and at least one major brand in this category doesn't publish gallons at all - only the barrel's empty and full weight.

## Temperature range is two numbers, not one

The low end is how cold the unit can get, which is what most buyers focus on. The high end matters more than people expect: a unit with built-in heating can also run as a warm soak or contrast-therapy setup, while a cooling-only unit is locked to whatever the tap or ice delivers. A published range like 37-104°F spans both uses; a range that stops in the low 90s or has no stated top end is cooling-only.

An unchilled barrel has no temperature range to publish, because the water is whatever you put into it - a different category of product, not a missing spec.

## Chiller power decides recovery time, not resting temperature

Once a chiller brings a tub to its target temperature, HP and wattage mostly show up in how fast it recovers after the water warms - from getting in, ambient heat, or consecutive uses. A 0.5 HP chiller recovers faster than a 0.25 HP unit in the same volume. If you plunge once a day, this rarely matters; if several people use the same tub back-to-back, recovery time compounds.

Manufacturers publish cooling rate inconsistently: one states degrees-per-hour directly, another states horsepower only, and the unchilled barrel publishes neither.

## Insulation and electrical requirement decide where it can go

Insulation thickness, published in inches for two of these three, determines how much energy the chiller spends fighting outside temperature - it matters most in an uninsulated garage or outdoors in winter. A thicker shell means the chiller cycles less to hold its setpoint.

Electrical requirement is a placement constraint as much as a spec. Every chilled unit here calls for a dedicated 120V/15A circuit, not one shared with other equipment - worth checking against your breaker panel before assuming a unit plugs into the nearest outlet. The unchilled barrel draws no power, which is one reason it can go anywhere a hose can reach.

## Matching the specs to your space

- **Setting up in an unheated garage or outdoors through winter** - insulation thickness and chiller HP are the specs to optimise, since both determine how hard the system works against ambient cold.
- **You want cold and warm from the same unit** - check that the published temperature range extends into heating territory (100°F+), not just down to the mid-30s.
- **Not ready to commit to a powered chiller** - an unchilled, ice-fillable tub is a real published category, not a downgrade, and the only one of the three with no electrical requirement.
- **Multiple people using it back-to-back** - chiller recovery rate (°F per hour, where published) matters more than the coldest temperature the unit can technically reach.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a dedicated electrical circuit for a chilled cold plunge?**

Manufacturers of chilled units here specify a dedicated 120V/15A circuit rather than a shared one. That's a stated requirement, not a suggestion - a chiller sharing a circuit with other high-draw equipment is a common way to trip a breaker mid-cycle, which is disruptive for a device meant to hold a steady temperature.

**Why doesn't every listing show a gallon capacity?**

Some manufacturers, particularly for simpler barrel-style tubs, publish weight (empty and full) instead of volume, and don't state shell material either. That's a real gap in the published spec sheet - when a figure isn't published, the honest answer is that it isn't published, not a number estimated from photos or similar products.

**Is a higher-horsepower chiller always better?**

Not necessarily. A higher-HP chiller recovers temperature faster after use, which matters if the tub sees consecutive sessions or sits somewhere cold. A single user in a temperature-controlled room will simply wait longer for a lower-powered chiller to reach and recover the same setpoint - a real trade-off, not a defect.
