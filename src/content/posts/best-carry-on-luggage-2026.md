Carry-on luggage looks like a solved problem until you're standing at the gate with a bag that either won't fit the sizer or is already falling apart after two trips. We compared three carry-ons that represent very different bets: a budget hardside spinner, a lightweight softside workhorse, and a premium hardside case built to last years. Here's how they actually differ once you look past the marketing photos.

## Amazon Basics 21-Inch Hardside Expandable Spinner Carry-On

This is the case most people buy when they need a carry-on this week and don't want to think about it much. The polycarbonate shell shrugs off the scuffs and dings that would show immediately on fabric luggage, and the expansion zipper adds real room for the flight home when you've picked up souvenirs. Based on owner reviews, it holds up fine for occasional flyers — a few trips a year rather than a weekly commute.

Where it shows its price is in the details: the spinner wheels aren't as smooth as pricier sets, and there's no interior organization beyond a basic zippered divider. For a case built to this budget, though, those are reasonable trade-offs rather than complaints.

## Travelpro Maxlite 5 Expandable Carry-On Spinner

Travelpro built its name selling luggage to flight crews, and the Maxlite 5 is the brand's answer to "lightest bag that won't fall apart." At roughly 7.8 pounds empty, it leaves more of the airline's weight allowance for what you actually pack, and the ballistic nylon shell has enough give to squeeze into overhead bins that a rigid hardside case might not fit.

The trade-off is protection: softside luggage flexes under pressure, which is good for fitting into bins but less good for anything fragile packed near the outside. It's also worth checking your airline's exact sizer before buying, since the exterior pockets add a small amount of bulk to the stated dimensions.

## Away The Carry-On

Away built a whole travel brand around one well-designed carry-on, and the case still holds up on specs: a hard, scratch-resistant polycarbonate shell, spinner wheels that roll smoothly in any direction, and an interior compression pad that helps flatten clothes so more fits. Away also backs it with a lifetime warranty and sells replacement wheels and handles directly, which matters for a bag you plan to keep for years.

The catch is straightforward — it costs more than four times the Amazon Basics option to do the same fundamental job of holding your clothes on a plane. It also doesn't expand the way cheaper hardside cases do, so what you see in the dimensions is the maximum you get.

## Bottom line

If you fly a few times a year and want something reliable without overthinking it, the Amazon Basics spinner covers the basics at a fraction of the cost. Frequent flyers who care most about weight and durability under real wear should look at the Travelpro Maxlite 5. If you want a case built to last a decade and don't mind paying for materials and warranty support, the Away carry-on is the one built for that job.

## Frequently asked questions

**What size counts as a "carry-on" for most US airlines?**
Most US carriers cap carry-ons around 22 x 14 x 9 inches including wheels and handles, though budget airlines like Spirit and Frontier use smaller limits closer to 18-20 inches for a free personal item. Always check your specific airline's page before flying, since sizers at the gate are stricter than the stated dimensions suggest.

**Is a hardside or softside carry-on better?**
Hardside cases protect contents better from crushing and show fewer visible scuffs, while softside bags flex to fit into tight overhead bins and typically have more exterior pocket organization. Neither is objectively better — it depends on whether you prioritize protection or flexibility.

**Do expensive carry-ons actually last longer than budget ones?**
Based on owner reviews and manufacturer warranty terms, pricier cases like the Away often use thicker-gauge polycarbonate and offer longer or lifetime warranties with replaceable parts, which matters if you travel frequently over many years. For occasional travelers, a budget case often lasts long enough that the cost difference isn't worth it.
