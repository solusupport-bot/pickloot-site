Automatic feeders split into two mechanisms: hopper feeders that dispense a scheduled portion into a bowl, and microchip-gated feeders that control which pet can reach a bowl that's already there. Four published specs tell you which type, and which model, fits your situation: food capacity, meals per day, battery backup, and connectivity. Each changes something concrete about how the feeder behaves once it's running unattended.

## Food capacity measures reserve, not control

The capacity figure — 5 liters, 24 cups, 400 milliliters — is how much food sits in the machine, not how much reaches your pet at once or how controlled that amount is. A large hopper means fewer refills and less risk of running dry while you travel. A small bowl, like the 400 mL SureFeed holds, isn't a reserve at all — you fill it fresh like a normal bowl, and the feeder's job is deciding who's allowed to eat from it.

Comparing hopper liters to bowl milliliters side by side is misleading on its own, since they measure different things. Match the number to the mechanism first, then to how long you need the feeder to run unattended.

## Meals per day and portion size set the schedule's granularity

A hopper feeder's meals-per-day figure, paired with its portion range, is what lets you replicate a vet-recommended schedule instead of approximating it. PetSafe's 1/8 cup to 4 cups spans a small cat to a large dog across up to 12 meals; PETLIBRO's roughly 20 mL portions, stacked up to 50 times a meal, suit frequent "grazing" feeding more than one large meal.

The microchip feeder has no meals-per-day figure published, because it doesn't schedule dispensing — it unlocks continuous access to a bowl you've already filled, gated to the registered pet. If your goal is portion control, that's a hopper-feeder spec to compare; if it's stopping a dog from eating the cat's food, meals-per-day isn't the number you need.

## Battery backup protects against outages, not daily use

On both hopper feeders the battery slot is explicitly a backup: each runs on AC power day to day, and D-cell batteries exist so a scheduled meal still dispenses through a power cut, not so the unit runs cordless. PETLIBRO states that Wi-Fi, the camera and remote control all go offline on battery power — the schedule keeps running, but you lose visibility until power returns. PetSafe publishes the same backup provision without a runtime figure.

SureFeed flips this: it runs on battery as its normal power source, with a published 6-month battery life, rather than staying plugged in — no outlet needed near the bowl, but a battery change on a fixed calendar instead of an occasional backup swap.

## Connectivity decides what you can do remotely, and what costs extra

Both hopper feeders connect to home Wi-Fi and a phone app, which lets you adjust a schedule, trigger a manual feeding, or get a low-food alert remotely. PETLIBRO adds a built-in camera to that connection; PetSafe adds Alexa voice control instead. Neither publishes a way to monitor the feeder without the app and a network connection.

SureFeed routes through a separate Hub rather than connecting the feeder to Wi-Fi directly, and that Hub is sold on its own unless bundled with the feeder. Its one published number — connecting up to 10 devices — counts Sure Petcare products per Hub, not pets.

## Matching the specs to your pet's feeding routine

- **You need fewer refills over a trip or busy week** — food capacity is the spec to optimise; a larger hopper buys more days unattended.
- **You're managing a specific schedule, like small frequent meals** — meals-per-day and portion-size range together, since both the count and the increment size need to fit your plan.
- **Power outages are a real risk where you live** — battery backup, keeping in mind only the schedule is guaranteed; camera and app access may still drop.
- **The real problem is one pet stealing another's food** — that's an access problem, not a capacity or schedule one, and the microchip-gated mechanism is built for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a hopper feeder keep dispensing meals if my Wi-Fi goes down?**

Yes — on both hopper models the schedule is stored on the device, so it keeps running without an internet connection. You lose remote control and alerts: no schedule changes, manual feeds, or jam notifications until the connection (and, on battery power, potentially the unit itself) is back.

**Can a microchip feeder stop one pet from eating another's food?**

That's specifically what it's built for, and it's something a timed hopper feeder can't do — a hopper dispenses into a bowl on schedule regardless of which animal gets there first. The microchip or RFID-tag feeder only opens access to the registered pet, a mechanism difference the spec table can't show as a number but that matters more than any published figure if that's your actual problem.

**How fresh does kibble stay sitting in a sealed hopper for days?**

Manufacturers address this with sealing rather than a shelf-life figure: PETLIBRO describes its hopper as quadruple-sealed and recommends replacing the included desiccant packet roughly every two weeks. Neither hopper feeder here publishes a maximum number of days food should sit before dispensing, so treat the desiccant schedule as the practical guide.
