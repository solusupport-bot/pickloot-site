Electric bikes all show a wattage number on the box, and it's tempting to treat that as the whole story. It isn't. Four published specs actually separate one e-bike from another in daily use: how the motor is rated, how much energy the battery holds, what range figure the manufacturer claims, and which speed class the bike is tuned to. None of them mean quite what the marketing copy implies, so here's what each one changes and where it stops mattering.

## Motor power tells you how it climbs, not how fast it goes

A hub motor's wattage rating (nominal, sometimes with a separate peak figure) describes how much force it can put into the rear wheel, not the bike's top speed - that's set electronically, separately, and covered below. A mid-drive motor like the Bosch Performance Line CX is rated differently: manufacturers publish torque in newton-meters instead of a headline wattage, because torque multiplied through the bike's gearing is what actually moves a loaded bike up a grade. That's why a "250 W nominal" mid-drive can out-climb a "500 W" hub motor despite the lower wattage number - the two figures aren't measuring the same thing, and comparing them directly is the single most common misread of an e-bike spec sheet.

## Battery capacity sets the energy budget, not the range you'll get

Watt-hours (Wh) is voltage multiplied by amp-hours, and it's the one figure that lets you compare energy storage across bikes on equal terms - when it's published. One bike in this set doesn't list a Wh figure at all, which means you can't check its manufacturer's range claim against a capacity number the way you can for the other two; you're relying on the range claim alone. A bigger Wh number generally means a longer ride before recharging, but how far that energy actually takes you depends on the next spec.

## Max range figures are ceilings, not averages

Manufacturer range numbers are almost always measured under favorable conditions: flat ground, the lowest assist level, a rider near the low end of the weight range, and mild weather. Real-world range at a mid or high assist setting, into wind, or with a heavier rider is commonly well under the published figure - riders and reviewers frequently report 40-60% of the claimed number under normal commuting use. Read the published range as the best case the bike can produce, not a number you should plan a route around without margin.

## Top speed is set by class, and by whether there's a throttle

In the US, e-bikes are grouped into classes: Class 1 is pedal-assist only up to 20 mph, Class 2 adds a throttle capped at 20 mph, and Class 3 allows pedal-assist up to 28 mph. This classification, not price, is what caps top speed - a budget hub-motor bike is often tuned to the 28 mph Class 3 ceiling because it's an easy marketing spec, while a pricier mid-drive commuter bike may deliberately stay at Class 1 with no throttle at all so it remains legal on multi-use paths that restrict faster classes. A higher top speed is not automatically the "better" spec; it's a different legal category with different access rules.

## Matching the specs to your commute

- **Hills or a loaded rack are the constraint** - prioritize motor torque (Nm) over wattage; a mid-drive's gearing advantage matters more here than a bigger hub-motor number.
- **Charging access is limited** - battery Wh and the bike's charge time matter more than range alone, since you need to know the energy budget you're working with, not just the distance claim.
- **Daily distance is long and recharging mid-day isn't realistic** - max range is the spec to lean on, but treat the published figure as a ceiling and look for real-world range reports before assuming you'll hit it.
- **You ride on shared paths with a posted speed-class limit** - top speed class and throttle presence decide where you're legally allowed to ride, independent of everything else on the spec sheet.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher wattage motor mean a faster top speed?**

No. Top speed on a road-legal e-bike is electronically capped by its class (typically 20 or 28 mph) regardless of motor wattage. A bigger motor shows up in acceleration and hill-climbing ability, not in how fast the bike will ultimately go.

**Why doesn't every manufacturer publish battery capacity in watt-hours?**

Some list only a claimed range or an amp-hour figure without the voltage needed to calculate Wh yourself. When that happens, you're comparing manufacturer-to-manufacturer range claims rather than the underlying energy capacity, which is a weaker comparison - it's worth noting in the spec table as "Not published" rather than guessing.

**If a spec sheet says a bike is Class 3, can I ride it anywhere?**

Not necessarily. State law sets the class definitions, but individual trail systems, parks, and bike paths can and do restrict which classes are allowed locally, sometimes down to Class 1 only. That's outside what any manufacturer's spec sheet can tell you, so it's worth checking with the specific trail or path operator before assuming a class-legal bike is welcome everywhere.
