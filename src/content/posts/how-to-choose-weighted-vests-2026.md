Rucking - walking with a loaded vest - has moved from military training circuits into mainstream fitness routines this year, and the vests being sold for it look more alike than they behave. Once you set aside styling, four published specs account for most of the real difference: how much weight the vest can carry, how that weight is loaded in, what the shell is made of, and how it's sized. This guide explains what each spec changes, then lines up three vests side by side so you can match the numbers to how you actually plan to load one.

## Weight capacity: the number that isn't always a single number

Some vests publish one maximum. Others don't publish a total at all, because the vest is really a carrier and the weight comes from plates you buy separately - in that case, the spec that matters is the plate-weight range the carrier accepts, not a figure on the vest itself. A vest with a wide published range (40-150 lb, say) is describing a family of versions, not one vest that magically holds all of those weights at once; check which specific version you're looking at. A vest with fixed loaded weights (14/20/30 lb) has already made that decision for you, which is a feature if you don't want to think about progression, and a limit if you do.

## Load type sets how the weight behaves on your body

How the weight is held changes how it moves as you walk. Sandbag pouches and loose fill shift slightly with each stride, which some people find helps distribute load and others find annoying. Rigid plates - whether standard SAPI-cut steel or a manufacturer's proprietary shape - stay fixed against your back and don't redistribute, which is closer to a fixed backpack feel. Proprietary plate shapes (like a curved plate contoured to the torso) are also a lock-in: you can only resupply weight from that same manufacturer, where a standard plate-carrier accepts plates from multiple brands.

## Shell material determines heat and durability, not capacity

None of these materials affect how much weight the vest holds - that's set by the load type above. What the shell changes is comfort over a long walk and how the vest ages. Breathable mesh exteriors run cooler but generally aren't built for abrasion against gear or rough surfaces. Heavier woven fabrics like 600D polyester or 500D/210D Cordura are rated for sustained abrasion and rough use, which matters more if the vest is going into a backpack, dragged through brush, or used daily rather than for occasional gym sessions.

## Sizing decides whether the fit is adjustable or chosen upfront

A one-size-fits-all vest relies entirely on straps and a cummerbund to fit a range of torso sizes, and the manufacturer is telling you the adjustment range is doing the real work. A vest sold in discrete sizes (S/L, for instance) is telling you the panel dimensions themselves change between sizes, with only the straps as extra adjustment - which generally fits closer to actual torso size for someone at either end of the range, at the cost of having to pick correctly before buying.

## Matching the specs to your training

- **You want room to add weight over months, not weeks** - prioritize a published capacity range wide enough that you won't outgrow the vest, and check whether "capacity" on that listing means one vest or a family of versions.
- **You already have a home gym with plates** - a plate-carrier style vest with a published plate-compatibility spec (rather than a built-in weight) lets you reuse what you own.
- **You're rucking outdoors for distance, not lifting in a gym** - a fixed-load vest with a rigid, closely fitted shell is the design built for that use case, not a flexibility gap.
- **You run warm or ruck in heat** - the shell material spec matters more than either of the others; a breathable mesh exterior is the one to check for first.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a heavier weighted vest burn more calories?**

Manufacturers don't publish calorie figures because they depend on your body weight, pace, and terrain, not just the vest's load - which is exactly the kind of number a spec sheet can't give you. The load type and how it sits on your body affects how sustainable a given weight feels over distance, which indirectly affects how long you can keep the pace up.

**Can I put my own weight plates in a "plate carrier" style vest?**

Only if the plate dimensions match what the carrier is built for. A carrier that lists compatibility with a named cut (SAPI, for example) or specific brands will fit those shapes; a proprietary curved-plate design generally won't accept a flat standard plate at all. Always check the published plate-compatibility spec before assuming any plate will fit.

**Why does one vest list a weight range and another list nothing?**

Some vests are sold as a single finished product with the weight built in, so the manufacturer publishes a maximum. Others are sold as an empty carrier meant to be paired with plates bought separately, so there's no total to publish - the number that matters instead is the plate-weight range the carrier is rated to hold, which is a different spec entirely.
