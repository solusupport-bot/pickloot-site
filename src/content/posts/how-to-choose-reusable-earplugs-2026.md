Reusable earplugs all promise "protection without silence," and side by side in a drawer they look nearly identical. What actually differs is written in the fine print: the certified noise reduction rating, the material molded into the tip, how many sizes ship in the box, and what accessories come bundled with it. Those four published specs account for most of what you'll notice after wearing a pair for a week, so here's what each one actually changes.

## Noise reduction rating is a lab ceiling, not a room number

NRR (Noise Reduction Rating) is the US standard figure, measured in a lab with test subjects fitted for an ideal seal. It's a decibel figure on a logarithmic scale, so the gap between an 8 dB and a 16 dB rating is a large perceptual difference, not a small one. What the number doesn't tell you is how it holds up outside a lab: several brands publish a second figure alongside the certified NRR precisely because the two diverge. Eargasm lists 16 dB NRR next to a separately stated 21 dB "expected" reduction; Vibes lists 15 dB NRR next to a 22 dB "average" figure across frequencies. Both gaps exist because the certified test assumes a seal most people don't get on the first try. Treat NRR as the number you approach with a correct fit, not one guaranteed the moment you put the plug in.

## Material determines whether you can wear these for hours

All three products here are silicone in some form — "reusable silicone," "hypoallergenic silicone," "soft silicone" — rather than the open-cell foam sold in bulk packs. Silicone reshapes around the ear canal instead of compressing and slowly expanding the way foam does, which is also why all three are described as washable with soap and water rather than disposable. The "hypoallergenic" distinction one manufacturer publishes matters mainly if you've had a reaction to standard silicone or to dyes used in colored tips; it isn't a noise-reduction spec at all.

## Ear tip sizing decides whether the certified number is reachable

NRR is only achieved with a sealed fit, and a seal depends on picking a tip that matches your ear canal. This is where the three diverge most: one ships two shell sizes, one ships three, and one ships seven combined silicone and foam options. More sizes mean more chances at a seal on an unusual canal shape, at the cost of more small pieces to track. It's also the spec a table can't fully answer on its own — a sizing chart is a starting point, not a guarantee, which is why every brand compared here backs its earplugs with a return window.

## Included accessories change the noise floor without changing the plug itself

A removable Mute attachment that adds decibels on demand behaves differently from a filter that's pre-installed and baked into the certified figure from the start — one gives you two settings in a single plug, the other gives you one fixed rating out of the box. The carrying case matters less for noise reduction and more for hygiene: a plug that rides loose in a bag pocket between uses picks up lint and dust that a case keeps out.

## Matching the specs to your day

- **You need to keep talking while blocking a loud room** — the lowest base NRR plus a removable Mute accessory gives two levels in one plug, instead of committing to a single fixed rating.
- **You're at concerts, on a factory floor, or around loud machinery most days** — the single highest certified NRR is the spec to prioritize over accessory count.
- **Your ear canals run small or unusually shaped** — more included sizes means more chances at a real seal before you're relying on a return window.
- **You don't want extra parts to lose** — fewer components is the simpler spec to manage, not automatically the weaker one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher NRR always mean the earplugs feel quieter?**

Not necessarily. NRR is measured in a lab with a fitted seal, and manufacturers themselves sometimes publish a separate real-world figure precisely because the certified number and the lived experience diverge. A correctly sized 15 dB plug that actually seals can feel quieter than a poorly fitted 16 dB one.

**How do I clean reusable silicone earplugs?**

All three manufacturers here recommend washing with mild soap and water and drying fully before the next use, and none recommend alcohol or solvents, which can degrade silicone over time. Replace the tips if you notice cracking, discoloration, or a seal that no longer holds.

**Will these work if I already wear hearing aids?**

That's not something a spec sheet can answer — it depends on your ear canal shape and your specific hearing aid style, not the published NRR figure. It's worth checking a brand's return window (several run 100 days) before committing to a pair, and checking with an audiologist if you're already managing hearing loss.
