On a shelf, packing cubes look like one product sold at three prices: a zippered fabric box with a mesh panel. The makers' own product pages disagree on four points — the shell fabric, whether there is a compression mechanism, whether water resistance is claimed, and what comes in the box. Those four published details account for most of the difference in how a set behaves over years of travel.

## Shell fabric decides how the set ages

Three fabric descriptions appear across these options and they are not interchangeable. Veken publishes twill polyester with mesh panels. Gonex publishes ripstop polyester. Peak Design publishes a 70D recycled ripstop.

Ripstop is a weave rather than a coating: a grid of heavier threads run through the fabric so a puncture stops at the next grid line instead of running across the panel. That matters for the failure mode packing cubes actually have — a snag on a zipper pull or a suitcase latch turning into a tear. Twill has no such grid.

Denier, where it is published, describes thread weight rather than strength, though the two correlate; 70D is a light technical fabric. Two of the three makers publish no denier at all — not evidence of a weak fabric, only a spec you cannot compare.

Where it stops mattering: if the cubes live inside a hard-shell suitcase and come out twice a year, the weave is close to irrelevant. Abrasion happens to cubes that are loaded and unloaded often.

## Compression is a mechanism, not an adjective

The word "compression" on a product page should point at a physical part. On the Gonex set it does: the maker describes double zippers, where a second zipper line draws the cube's panels closer once it is packed. Peak Design describes an expansion and compression zip alongside an internal divider. Veken's own page describes no compression feature — that set is organisation only.

This is the spec that decides which problem a set can solve. A cube with no compression zip cannot reduce the volume of what you pack; it only keeps it sorted, and the fabric itself adds a little bulk. A compression zip removes air from soft items, which in practice means clothing, and does nothing measurable for shoes or anything rigid.

Where it stops mattering: if your bag closes with room to spare, the compression zip is an extra step at packing time with no payoff.

## Water resistance describes the fabric, not the closure

Veken states that its fabric has water-resistant properties. Peak Design describes its shell as weatherproof. Gonex publishes no water-resistance claim for this set. None publishes a rating — no hydrostatic head figure, no IP-style number — so both words are descriptions rather than measurements.

A treated shell sheds a leaking bottle or condensation inside a bag. It is not a dry bag, and a standard coil zipper is the first place water enters regardless of what the panel is made of. Read these claims as "damp travel is survivable", not "the contents stay dry".

## Set contents change what you have to buy

The three options are packaged differently, which makes per-piece comparisons misleading. Veken publishes a six-piece set of large, medium and small cubes plus a specialty bag. Gonex publishes six pieces as one large, two medium, two small and a laundry bag. Peak Design sells cubes individually in Small, Medium and Large.

Individual sizing lets you buy two mediums and nothing else. A fixed set usually includes a size you will never open. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.

## Matching the specs to your luggage

- **Your bag already closes** — compression is the spec you can ignore; fabric and set contents are the real choice.
- **You travel carry-on only** — the compression zip is the one mechanism that changes what fits, and the maker has to describe it explicitly.
- **You repack every couple of days** — shell fabric and zipper construction, because those are the parts that see the wear.
- **You want exactly two cubes, not six** — individually sold sizes, since a fixed set decides the sizes for you.
- **You check bags in wet climates** — a stated water-resistant or weatherproof shell, knowing no maker here publishes a rating behind the word.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do packing cubes save space, or only organise?**

Only cubes with a compression mechanism reduce volume. Plain cubes corral items so the bag stays sorted, and the fabric adds a little bulk, so a non-compression set slightly reduces what fits. Check the maker's page for a second zipper line before assuming a set compresses anything.

**What sizes should I buy for a carry-on?**

Most carry-on packers end up with a mix: a large cube for bulky knitwear, a medium for everyday clothing, a small for underwear and accessories. Fixed sets are built around that assumption. If you know you want only two sizes, individually sold cubes avoid paying for the ones that stay in the cupboard.

**Are cubes worth it for short trips?**

They help at any length, but the effect is clearer the more often you repack. For a single overnight bag, folding is about as fast. For a week away, or a multi-stop trip where the bag is opened daily, cubes remove the repacking step that turns a suitcase into a pile.
