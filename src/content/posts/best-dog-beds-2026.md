Dog beds photograph identically and describe themselves in the same adjectives, but the foam under a thin bed and a thick one is doing different work. Five published specs account for most of the difference: the foam construction, the bed height, the dog weight it was engineered for, the cover care, and the warranty. This guide explains what each changes — and which ones the category tends not to publish at all.

## Foam construction is the spec this category publishes least

Of the three beds here, one publishes its layer structure in full — viscoelastic memory foam over a polyurethane support base — one lists four named foam types without saying how they are assembled, and one publishes no construction detail at all.

What construction changes is where your dog's weight ends up. A single slab of soft memory foam compresses under a heavy dog until the dog is effectively on the floor; a firmer support layer underneath stops that. Convoluted (egg-crate) foam is lighter for the same nominal height, because much of its volume is air. None of that shows in a photograph.

## Bed height is not the same as usable support

The height figure is the outside dimension, and on a bolstered or sofa-style bed most of it is the raised sides. The Casper listing makes the gap explicit: 6.5 to 7.5 inches tall by size, with a flat inner sleeping area of 3.5 inches. The Big Barker beds are a flat 7 inches across all three sizes, and that is the whole surface.

What matters is the foam between your dog's hip or elbow and the floor. For a light dog, three inches is usually enough that nothing bottoms out. For a heavy dog, thin foam compresses at the pressure point and the bed stops doing its job. So read the height figure, then check whether it describes the bolster or the sleeping surface.

## Weight guidance tells you what the foam was engineered for

Only one of the three publishes a per-size weight ceiling — 30, 60 and 90 pounds. The other two publish dimensions but no weight rating, and one gives a sizing rule instead: a bed three to four inches longer than the dog.

Those answer different questions. A length rule says whether the dog fits on the bed; a weight rating says whether the foam holds up under the dog. A 100-pound dog fits comfortably on a bed rated to 90 pounds and still compresses the foam faster than it was designed for. Under about 40 pounds the spec stops binding.

## Cover care and warranty decide how long you keep it

All three publish a removable, machine-washable cover, so that is close to table stakes. The variation is in the instructions, where two specify cold water and low or no heat. Heat shrinks a cover until it no longer fits back over the foam, so the drying line matters more than it reads.

Warranty is where the numbers diverge most sharply: 60 days, one year, ten years. The last is specifically a no-flatten warranty, published as the foam retaining at least 90 percent of its shape. That is not a performance measurement, but it is a manufacturer stating in writing what it expects the foam to do over time.

## Matching the specs to your dog

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest in your house.

- **The dog is heavy, or lands hard on one joint** — foam construction and usable sleeping-surface height, since compression at the pressure point is the failure mode.
- **The dog is small, or still growing** — sizing and cover care; the support spec is not binding yet.
- **You are not sure the dog will use a floor bed** — cover washability and a short return window matter more than foam depth.
- **The bed sits in a high-traffic or muddy spot** — cover care, and specifically the drying instruction rather than the wash cycle.
- **You expect to keep the bed for years** — warranty length, the only published figure that speaks to the foam after the first year.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does "orthopedic" mean anything specific on a dog bed listing?**

Not as a regulated term — any manufacturer can use it. It usually signals a solid foam base rather than loose polyester fill, which is a real structural difference. Treat the word as a prompt to check the published construction and height, not as a specification in itself.

**How do I size a bed for a dog that sprawls versus one that curls up?**

Measure the dog nose to tail-base in the position they actually sleep in, then add the manufacturer's margin — three to four inches where one is published. A curler can use a smaller bed with bolsters to lean against; a sprawler needs the flat interior dimension, which on a bolstered bed is meaningfully smaller than the outside one.

**Are washable covers enough to manage dog smell, or does the foam hold odor?**

Covers handle surface dirt and most of the smell, since that is where fur and dander collect. Foam absorbs odor over time if liquid reaches it, which is what a waterproof inner liner is for — some beds publish one, some do not. Check for a published liner before the first accident rather than after.
