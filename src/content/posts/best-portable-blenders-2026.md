Three products sold as portable blenders can work in completely different ways: one plugs into the wall and is portable only in that the cup travels, one runs off a battery, and one is a small countertop machine with a travel lid. Four published specs tell you which is which - the power source, the peak motor figure, the blending capacity as opposed to the vessel size, and, for battery models, blends per charge.

## Power source is the first fork, and it changes every other spec

Two of the three models here are corded, which means "portable" describes the cup rather than the appliance: the Magic Bullet publishes a 29.5 in cord and the Ninja a 5.62 lb base, so both are counter appliances whose tumbler goes out the door. The Beast GO Cordless publishes an 11.1 V three-cell battery and a USB-C port with a charge time under two hours, which is what makes it usable where there is no outlet.

Read this column first, because it decides whether the rest of the table applies. Blends per charge is meaningless for a mains-powered unit, and peak wattage is not published for the battery one.

## Peak watts describe a ceiling, not the power you use

Peak wattage is the maximum the motor draws under load, usually for a moment while it bites into something frozen. Manufacturers print it because it is the largest number available, and it is genuinely comparable between corded models: Ninja publishes 1,200 peak watts against the Magic Bullet's 250 W motor base, and that gap is real.

It stops being comparable the moment a battery is involved. Beast publishes no wattage for the GO Cordless, only the battery voltage - and volts and watts are not the same measurement, so there is no honest way to line an 11.1 V battery up against a 1,200-watt peak.

## Blending capacity is smaller than the vessel you see

The volume printed on a cup is the total volume, which includes the headroom the contents need to circulate. Beast is unusually explicit and publishes both figures: a 670 ml (23 oz) vessel with a 500 ml (17 oz) blending capacity, so roughly a quarter of what you see is headroom rather than smoothie. Ninja publishes a 26 oz tumbler without a separate maximum fill line, and Magic Bullet publishes the contents of its eleven-piece set - a tall cup, a short cup and a party mug - without volumes for any of them.

Where it stops mattering: for a single 12 oz smoothie, all three sit comfortably inside their limits. The figure binds when you are making a meal-replacement portion, because that is when headroom starts eating into the serving.

## Blends per charge is what limits a cordless blender's day

For a battery model this is the spec that decides whether the machine fits your week. Beast publishes approximately 13 blending cycles of 30 seconds each per full charge on its motor base page, and up to 15 cycles on its product overview - the two figures do not agree, which is worth knowing before you plan around either. Charging is published as under two hours over USB-C.

For the corded pair the cell reads "not applicable" rather than "unlimited" - the same fact from the other direction: they run as long as you like, wherever there is a socket.

## Matching the specs to your desk, gym bag or kitchen

Rather than one pick, the useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **There is no outlet where you blend** - the power source column settles it alone.
- **Frozen fruit or ice most days** - peak wattage, the only published proxy, and corded-only.
- **You make a full meal-replacement portion** - blending capacity rather than vessel volume.
- **You blend several times between charges** - blends per charge, taking the lower published figure.
- **Cleanup is the thing you skip** - the dishwasher-safe column; every motor base is excluded.

## Frequently asked questions

**Which published spec tells me whether it will handle ice?**

Peak wattage is the closest comparable figure, and it exists for corded models only - the 1,200 peak watts Ninja publishes and the 250 W Magic Bullet motor base line up against each other. Ninja also publishes a dedicated ice crush program, which is a feature rather than a performance claim. Beast publishes neither a wattage figure nor an ice preset, so on this axis the spec sheet cannot answer the question.

**How long do portable blender batteries last before needing replacement?**

Manufacturers publish charge time and cycles per charge, not battery lifespan, so there is no spec to compare. As a general property of lithium-ion cells, capacity declines with repeated charge cycles rather than holding indefinitely, which makes a cordless blender's useful life partly a battery question. Beast publishes a two-year warranty on the GO Cordless motor base, the closest published figure to an answer.

**Is it safe to blend hot liquids in a personal blender?**

Generally no, unless the manufacturer specifically states otherwise. Sealed personal-blender cups can build pressure with hot contents, and the seal or the cup can fail. None of the three published spec sheets here lists hot blending as a supported use, so treat these as machines for cold or room-temperature ingredients.
