Every snow blower on a shelf promises to clear your driveway, and the marketing copy rarely explains why one costs twice what another does. Four published specs account for most of the real difference: clearing width, intake height, power source and stage type, and how far the machine can throw snow. This guide walks through what each one changes, then lays three models out side by side so you can match the numbers to your own driveway and climate.

## Clearing width decides how many passes your driveway takes

Clearing width is the swath the machine cuts in a single pass, measured at the housing's front edge. A 21-inch unit and a 30-inch unit moving at the same walking pace clear the same driveway in meaningfully different numbers of passes — roughly 30% more for the narrower machine over an equivalent area.

Width isn't free, though. A wider housing is also heavier and harder to maneuver around parked cars and tight side yards, so the spec that helps on a long driveway can be a liability on a short, obstacle-heavy one.

## Intake height is the ceiling on how deep a snowfall you can handle in one pass

Intake height is the published measurement for how tall a pile of snow the housing can accept before it starts pushing rather than lifting. The three models here range from 12 to 21 inches. Below that height, the machine works as advertised. Above it, you're plowing rather than clearing, and performance drops off — the auger scoops the bottom layer while the rest gets shoved forward.

Check this spec against your area's typical storm totals, not your driveway's size. A small driveway in a region that gets 18-inch dumps needs intake height more than it needs width.

## Power source and stage type set how much snow you can move — and how much upkeep you take on

These two specs move together. Single-stage machines use a spinning auger that touches the ground directly to scoop and throw snow in one motion; this is common on battery and corded electric units because the mechanism needs less torque. Two-stage machines add a second part, an impeller, that takes snow from the auger and throws it separately — what lets a two-stage gas engine handle wet, heavy snow that would stall a single-stage unit.

The trade-off is upkeep. A cordless single-stage machine has no gas to store and starts by pulling a trigger. A two-stage gas engine needs fuel and periodic oil changes, and — worth flagging directly — neither gas engine here publishes a horsepower figure; both manufacturers rate their engines only in cc displacement, a labeling choice rather than a missing number to estimate yourself.

## Throwing distance determines where the snow actually ends up

Throwing distance is published either as a single top figure or, on more detailed spec sheets, a controllable range. The range matters more than the maximum: a machine that throws snow 30 feet in one direction is fine on an open driveway, but not if your only clear spot is a flower bed 10 feet away that the chute can't be aimed down to without burying it.

One of these three manufacturers states its maximum distance in product description copy rather than a dedicated spec table — still manufacturer-published, just formatted differently than the other two.

## Matching the specs to your driveway

- **Driveway is short, snow rarely tops a few inches** — clearing width matters less than storage and startup; a cordless single-stage unit covers this with no fuel can needed.
- **Snowfall regularly exceeds 12-15 inches, or you get heavy, wet drifts** — check intake height and stage type first; a single-stage machine will push rather than lift past its rated height.
- **Cleared snow needs to land somewhere specific, not just "away"** — throwing distance and chute control decide this, and a published range beats a single top number.
- **You're clearing gravel or an uneven path** — stage type again: a single-stage auger contacts the ground and can fling loose material, where a two-stage impeller sits above it.
- **Storage space or fuel handling is the real constraint** — power source is the spec to optimize, since a battery system removes both the gas can and engine maintenance.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a single-stage snow blower damage a gravel driveway?**

It can fling loose gravel along with the snow, because its auger makes direct contact with the ground to scoop. Two-stage machines keep the auger housing slightly elevated and use a separate impeller to throw snow, which is the configuration manufacturers generally recommend for gravel or unpaved surfaces.

**Why don't these gas models publish a horsepower number?**

Troy-Bilt and Ariens both rate these two engines only in cubic-centimeter displacement, not horsepower, on their own product pages. That's a manufacturer labeling choice rather than a missing spec — cc figures are still comparable to each other, just not directly to a horsepower rating from a brand that does publish one.

**Does a bigger engine mean the machine clears snow faster?**

Not directly. Engine size affects how much torque is available to keep the auger and impeller turning through dense snow without stalling, but clearing speed in practice tracks clearing width and walking pace more closely. A larger engine on a narrow housing won't clear a driveway faster than a wider housing on a smaller one.
