GPS pet trackers all look like the same small pod clipped to a collar, and the marketing copy leans on the same words - real-time, waterproof, escape alerts. What actually separates them is published in the spec sheet, not the product photo: the device's own weight, its waterproof rating, how long it runs between charges, and which network it depends on to report a location. This guide explains what each of those specs changes, then lines up three tiers from the same maker so you can compare the numbers directly.

## Weight matters more on a small pet than a large one

A tracker adds mass to whatever collar the pet already wears, and that addition is fixed in grams regardless of the animal underneath it. A few extra grams is a rounding error on a 70-pound dog and a much larger proportional addition on a 10-pound dog or a cat - which is why manufacturers publish a dedicated lightweight model rather than sizing one device for everyone. In this comparison the published weights run from 16 g up to 68 g, better than a fourfold spread between the lightest and heaviest options from the same brand.

## The waterproof rating tells you what kind of exposure it survives, not how tough it is overall

An IP68 rating means two published things under the standard IP code: dust-tight, and protected against continuous submersion beyond one meter of water. That covers rain, puddles, and a dog jumping in a lake. It does not, on its own, say anything about a direct jet of water at pressure - a hose-down, a pressure washer, or waves rather than still water. One model in this comparison publishes an additional rating alongside plain IP68, specifying resistance in both fresh and saltwater to 50 meters deep. That is a broader published claim than IP68 alone, and it is worth checking for specifically if the pet swims in the ocean rather than a backyard pool.

## Battery duration trades against how many networks the device is running at once

A tracker's power draw scales with what it is listening for. A device built to check in over a single cellular network sips less power than one built to automatically hand off between LTE, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and satellite. That is the pattern in this comparison: the two cellular-only models publish battery life in months, while the model with satellite fallback publishes battery life in days. The shorter figure on the higher tier is not a manufacturing shortcoming - maintaining a satellite link alongside a standard cellular connection is a genuinely higher power draw, and the published number reflects that directly.

## Network coverage decides where the tracker still works, not how accurate it is

A cellular-only tracker reports a location wherever its assigned carrier has service, and carrier coverage maps differ meaningfully block to block, especially outside cities. Satellite fallback, where it is published, exists specifically to close the gaps a carrier's towers don't reach - trailheads, backcountry, open water. The manufacturer describes this as an automatic handoff rather than a separate mode: the device connects to LTE when it can and switches to satellite only when cellular is out of reach. It is a backup path for dead zones, not a general accuracy upgrade over the cellular-only models.

## Matching the specs to your dog

- **Your pet is small, or a cat** - weight is the spec to optimise. Check the lightest published figure against what the pet already wears comfortably.
- **You live somewhere wet, or your dog swims often** - waterproof rating, specifically whether the listing publishes anything beyond plain IP68, like a jet or saltwater-rated figure.
- **You're not reliable about charging small devices** - battery duration. The published gap in this comparison runs from days to months, which is the difference between a weekly habit and a twice-a-year one.
- **You hike, camp, or live somewhere cell coverage is unreliable** - network coverage, specifically whether a satellite fallback is published at all.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a GPS tracker stop my dog from running off?**

No. These are location and alert devices, not containment systems. They report where the dog is and can send an alert when it crosses a boundary you set in the app, but nothing in the published specs physically restrains the animal - that's a different product category (a wired or wireless containment fence), and none of the three trackers here are built for it.

**Why does the most expensive tier have the shortest published battery life?**

Because it is maintaining more connections at once. The model with satellite fallback is rated for 2 to 3 days, against months for the two cellular-only models, because keeping a satellite link active alongside the standard LTE connection draws meaningfully more power. A shorter battery figure on a higher tier reflects what the added connectivity costs, not a weaker battery.

**Do I need my own phone plan for the tracker to work?**

No. Each tracker connects over its own built-in cellular connection through the manufacturer's own service, not your personal phone's data plan. The published "network coverage" spec (Verizon, AT&T, or T-Mobile in this comparison) refers to whose towers the tracker's own SIM uses - a separate account from your phone service entirely.
