Three carry-on cases can sit side by side looking like the same product in different finishes, and the marketing copy rarely separates them. Four published specs account for most of the practical difference: exterior dimensions including wheels, empty weight, shell material, and the warranty terms. This guide explains what each one changes in use, then lays three cases out side by side.

## Exterior dimensions are the only number the gate measures

Manufacturers publish two dimension sets, and the gap between them matters. Case dimensions describe the shell alone; exterior or overall dimensions add the wheels and the retracted handle. Airline sizers measure the second one, so that is the figure to compare — a case advertised as "21-inch" can measure 23 inches once its wheels are counted.

Most US carriers cap carry-ons near 22 x 14 x 9 inches, so two of the three cases here exceed the stated height on paper. Sizers have some tolerance and soft cases compress where rigid ones cannot, but a published height above the limit is a known risk rather than a surprise, and budget carriers enforce it hardest. Depth is usually what decides whether a case slides into a sizer at all.

## Empty weight decides how much of the allowance is left for clothes

Empty weight is subtracted from your allowance before you pack anything. On carriers that weigh cabin bags — common outside the US and on some budget fares — the spread between these three cases is over two pounds, which is roughly a pair of shoes.

Weight does not track price here. The mid-band softside case is the lightest at 5.4 lbs and the premium hardside case the heaviest at 7.5 lbs — a materials outcome, since a rigid polycarbonate shell thick enough to resist cracking weighs more than coated fabric over a frame. Empty weight stops mattering entirely if your airline only measures size, so check which rule applies first.

## Shell material is a trade between protection and give

The material line separates hardside from softside, and the two fail in opposite directions. Polycarbonate and polypropylene shells resist crushing and hide scuffs, but hold a fixed shape — a full hard case either fits the bin or does not. Coated polyester flexes into a bin a rigid case is turned away from, and carries exterior pockets hard shells cannot.

The trade runs the other way for anything fragile: a softside wall transmits pressure to whatever is packed against it, which is why makers of soft cases describe interior organisation rather than impact resistance.

Polypropylene and polycarbonate are not interchangeable either — polypropylene is cheaper and more flexible, polycarbonate stiffer and what premium hard cases use. No maker here publishes a shell thickness, so the material name is as far as the published comparison goes.

## Warranty terms differ in structure, not just length

"Lifetime" appears on two of these three warranties and means something different in each. One is a limited lifetime warranty on named components — wheels, zippers, handles. One is a repair programme covering labour for five years, continuing afterwards with the maker paying shipping. The third is a fixed 10-year global warranty.

What to compare is not the headline duration but which parts are covered, whether the remedy is repair or replacement, and whether wheels and handles can be ordered separately. Those are the components that fail first on a case that flies often, so a warranty excluding them covers the least likely failure.

## Matching the specs to your travel

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **You fly budget carriers or international routes** — exterior dimensions, since those sizers are enforced literally.
- **Your airline weighs cabin bags** — empty weight, because it comes off the allowance before you pack.
- **You pack fragile items** — shell material, the only spec here that addresses crushing.
- **You fly most weeks** — warranty structure and replaceable parts, since wheels and handles wear out first.

## Frequently asked questions

**What size counts as a "carry-on" for most US airlines?**

Most US carriers cap carry-ons around 22 x 14 x 9 inches including wheels and handles. Budget airlines such as Spirit and Frontier apply smaller limits closer to 18–20 inches for the free personal item. Check your specific airline's published dimensions before flying, and compare them against the exterior figure rather than the case figure, since gate sizers measure the wheels too.

**Is a hardside or softside carry-on better?**

Neither is better as a category — they trade against each other. Hard shells protect contents from crushing and hide scuffs; soft cases flex into tight bins and carry exterior pockets. The specs that tell you which you are getting are the shell material line and, indirectly, the weight, since rigid shells run heavier at the same size.

**Do the manufacturers publish how thick the shell is?**

No. All three publish the material name — polypropylene, coated polyester, polycarbonate — and none publishes a wall thickness or an impact rating, so shell durability cannot be compared numerically from published specs. Warranty scope is the closest published proxy, since it states which components the manufacturer expects to stand behind.
