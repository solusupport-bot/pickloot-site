Under-desk cable trays all photograph as the same steel basket, and the listings rarely lead with the part that decides whether one works on your desk. Four published specs do most of that work: the tray's dimensions, its weight capacity, how it attaches, and the desktop thickness it attaches to. This guide explains what each one physically changes, then lays three trays out side by side so you can check them against your own desk.

## Tray dimensions decide what actually fits inside

Length is the spec people compare and depth is the spec that matters. The usual job is to get a power strip off the floor, and a strip is roughly two inches deep before any plugs go in — so a three-inch-deep tray holds the strip and very little else, while a six-inch-deep tray holds the strip plus a coiled slack loop or a laptop brick beside it. Height behaves the same way: a 4.5-inch basket hides flat cable runs, a seven-inch one swallows an adapter standing on end.

Read depth and height first, against the largest single object you need out of sight, then length against how much of the desk span you want covered.

## Weight capacity is the spec that does not separate them

All three publish the same figure: 11 lbs. That is the standard published rating for a single-desk cable tray, and it sits well above what the contents actually weigh — a surge protector, two adapters and a USB hub together rarely reach four pounds.

So read it once to rule out an outlier, then set it aside. It matters again only if you plan to mount something genuinely heavy — a small UPS, a mini PC — at which point you are past what a cable tray publishes a rating for, and an under-desk shelf rated for the load is the honest comparison instead.

## Mounting method is the difference between permanent and reversible

Two of these screw into the underside of the desktop; one clamps on. Screws are stronger and position-free — the tray goes wherever you want on the underside, including dead center of a wide desk. The costs are a few permanent holes and a material requirement: wood or particle board, not glass and not thin metal.

A clamp needs no tools and leaves nothing behind, which is the whole point in a rental, on a glass top, or on a desk with resale value. The trade is placement. A clamp has to sit where it can grip an edge or a frame member, so it cannot always go where the cables already are.

## Desk thickness range is where clamp mounts succeed or fail

This is the spec most likely to make a tray unusable, and it is easy to skip. The clamp-mount tray here publishes a range of 0.2 to 1.97 inches. One screw-mount tray publishes a 0.7-inch minimum desktop thickness, because the screws need material to bite into. The third does not publish a figure at all.

Measure at the exact spot you intend to mount, not at the edge — plenty of desks taper. A one-inch top is inside the clamp's published range; a 2.5-inch solid top is outside it, and clamping force is not a variable you can adjust. An unpublished figure is not a warning sign, but it is the one axis you cannot check before the box arrives.

## Matching the specs to your desk

There is no single answer here, so the useful question is which constraint binds hardest on your setup.

- **You rent, or the desktop is glass** — mounting method is the only spec that matters, and a clamp is the version that needs no drilling.
- **A power strip plus two power bricks is the actual mess** — depth and height, not length. Six inches is the deepest published figure here.
- **The desktop is unusually thick, thin or tapered** — start with desk thickness range and let it eliminate options first.
- **You want cables invisible across a wide span** — length, and plan on two trays rather than hunting for one long enough.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will drilling into my desk damage it or void a warranty?**

Small screw holes in the underside of a desktop are cosmetic rather than structural, but check the warranty terms if the desk is recent — some manufacturers exclude damage from aftermarket mounting. The published minimum desktop thickness is the figure that tells you whether the screws have enough material to bite into. If either is a problem, a clamp mount avoids the question.

**Can I use these with a standing desk that changes height?**

Yes, and a screw-mounted tray is the simpler case because it travels with the desktop, so the tray-to-monitor distance never changes. The tray-to-outlet distance does, so route enough slack at the lifting column to cover the full travel. Clamps work too, provided they can grip an edge or frame member that is not part of the lifting mechanism.

**Do these scale to a gaming setup rather than an office desk?**

Up to a point, and the 11 lb rating is not the limit you hit first — volume is. A multi-monitor or multi-console desk generates more cable bulk than a 16- or 17-inch tray holds, which is why published length and depth matter more here than the weight figure. Measure the actual bundle to find out whether one tray covers it.
