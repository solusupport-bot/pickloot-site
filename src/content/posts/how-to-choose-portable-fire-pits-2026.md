Portable fire pits all promise the same backyard evening, and product photos make a plain steel bowl and a fan-assisted, app-adjacent unit look interchangeable. Four published specs account for most of the real difference: what fuel it burns, how much it weighs, how much space it needs once it's set up, and how much heat it's rated to put out. This guide explains what each one changes, then lays three models out side by side so you can match the numbers to your own yard or campsite.

## Fuel type decides what you're managing, not how big the flame gets

Propane burns through a valve at a controlled rate, which is what makes a fire pit like the Outland Firebowl instant to light and instant to shut off — turn the knob and the flame is gone, with no smoke smell left in your jacket. The cost of that convenience is a 20 lb tank and a hose to haul alongside it, and the tank has to go somewhere when you're not using it.

Wood-burning designs like the Solo Stove Bonfire trade that convenience for a fire that behaves like an actual fire: sparks, real radiant heat, and a need for dry fuel you either buy or split yourself. "Smokeless" wood designs use a double-wall airflow system to burn off more of the smoke than an open fire would, but they are not smoke-free — see the FAQ below. The BioLite FirePit+ sits in between: it still burns wood or charcoal, but a battery-powered fan forces extra air into the chamber, which measurably cuts visible smoke compared with an unassisted wood fire while adding a battery pack to the list of things you maintain.

## Weight is where "portable" starts and stops meaning something

The three published weights here span 19.8 lb to 26 lb — not a huge range, but it's the difference between a one-hand carry from the garage and a two-hand lift into a trunk. The BioLite, at 19.8 lb, is the lightest of the three specifically because it has no stand, no stone insert, and a thinner-gauge collapsible body. The Solo Stove's 25.1 lb figure is for the bundle with its stand and carry case included, which is part of why it isn't the lightest despite being the smallest in diameter.

Where it stops mattering: if the fire pit is going to live permanently on a patio and never travel farther than storage in the fall, weight is close to irrelevant. It binds hardest for anyone loading a fire pit into a car for every camping trip or tailgate.

## Size (unfolded) sets your fire's footprint and your clearance requirement

The diameter or footprint a manufacturer publishes tells you two things: how much fuel can burn at once, and how much clearance you need from a deck rail, fence, or overhanging branch. A 21-inch propane bowl and a 19.5-inch wood-burning one are close enough that clearance needs are similar; the BioLite's 27 x 13-inch oval footprint is wider in one direction and narrower in the other, which matters if you're placing it on a specific patio slab rather than open ground.

Folded dimensions matter separately from unfolded ones. The BioLite is the only one of the three published with a distinct folded height (10.5 inches) versus its unfolded height (15.8 inches), because it's designed to collapse for transport. The other two ship and store at their full footprint.

## BTU output is a spec only a metered fuel can publish

Outland publishes 58,000 BTU/hr for the Firebowl because propane flow can be measured precisely at the regulator — a fixed volume of gas per hour translates directly into a fixed heat output. Neither Solo Stove nor BioLite publishes a BTU figure for their wood- and charcoal-burning models, and that's not a missing spec so much as an unmeasurable one: how much heat a wood fire puts out depends on the species of wood, how dry it is, how it's stacked, and how much air is reaching it, all of which change fire to fire. A single number on a spec sheet would be misleading rather than helpful.

## Matching the specs to your yard or campsite

- **Fire restrictions apply where you camp or live** — fuel type is the spec to check first. Contained gas appliances are often treated differently from open wood flames under local burn bans, but the exemption depends on the specific ban, not on the fire pit itself.
- **It's going into a car for every outing** — weight and folded size together decide how much of your trunk it takes up and how it rides between uses.
- **You want the largest fire a stationary backyard setup can hold** — the unfolded footprint is what limits how much fuel is burning at once.
- **You need a specific, repeatable heat level, such as for cooking over it** — BTU output is the spec to compare, and currently only the propane option publishes one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are smokeless wood-burning fire pits actually smoke-free?**

No. "Smokeless" describes a double-wall airflow design that draws in secondary air to burn off more of the particulate than an open campfire would, which noticeably cuts visible smoke — it doesn't eliminate it. Wet or green wood, a stuffed-full chamber, and wind direction can all still produce a smoky fire regardless of the design.

**Can I use a propane fire pit during a local wood-burning ban?**

Often, but check first rather than assuming. Many air-quality and fire-danger restrictions specifically target open wood flames and exempt contained gas appliances, but the exact wording and exemptions come from whichever county or state agency issued the ban — not from the fire pit's own manufacturer. A product listing that calls something "compliant" during "most fire bans" is describing typical rules, not the rule that applies to your address that week.

**Why don't the wood-burning models list a BTU rating?**

BTU is a rate of heat output tied to a known, constant fuel flow — something a propane regulator can guarantee and an open wood or charcoal fire can't. Two fires built from the same model of fire pit can put out meaningfully different heat depending on the wood's moisture content and species, so no manufacturer can honestly print one fixed number for it. Treat "Not published" here as a property of the fuel, not a gap in the spec sheet.
