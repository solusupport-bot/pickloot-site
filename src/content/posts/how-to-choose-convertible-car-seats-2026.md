Convertible car seats mostly look the same in a product photo — a shell, a harness, a base — and marketing language like "safety-tested" doesn't help you tell them apart. Underneath, five published specs account for most of what differs: the rear- and forward-facing weight ranges, the separate LATCH weight limit, the number of harness positions, and the expiration date. This guide explains what each one changes and lays three models side by side.

## Weight ranges describe when you must switch, not how safe the seat is

A seat's rear-facing and forward-facing weight ranges set the outer bounds of each mode, not a safety verdict on the mode itself. Current guidance favors keeping a child rear-facing as long as the seat allows, so a higher ceiling — Graco's 50 lb versus Britax's 40 lb here — buys more time before turning the seat around, provided your child hasn't already outgrown it by height first (more on that below).

The forward-facing range matters less for which model to buy and more for how long one seat lasts afterward: all three here reach 65 lb, which covers most children into early elementary school.

## The LATCH weight limit is a separate ceiling from the seat's own weight range

LATCH lower anchors carry their own combined weight limit — child plus seat — often well below the number elsewhere on the spec sheet. This is clearest on the Clek Foonf: its seat belt install reaches 50 lb rear-facing and 65 lb forward-facing, but its rigid-LATCH connectors are rated only to 25 lb and 35 lb. Once a child passes that lower LATCH figure, the seat isn't outgrown — you simply reinstall it with the vehicle's own seat belt instead.

Britax does not publish a LATCH weight limit for the Boulevard ClickTight at all. That's a real gap for comparison purposes: it means you cannot plan an installation method around a spec sheet number and instead need to check the seat's printed labeling or manual directly.

## Harness positions determine how long one seat fits without buying another

"No-rethread" harness positions are the height settings you reach with a lever or dial, rather than by physically unthreading the harness straps and moving them to a different slot. More positions generally means finer adjustment as a child grows, which is part of why the Britax here — the mid-tier model — has more than double the Clek's count (14 versus 6). That is not a price-tier pattern so much as a design choice: the Clek's narrower harness range reflects a seat built around switching installation methods rather than maximizing one continuous growth window.

## Expiration dates are a materials decision, not a marketing gimmick

Every car seat has an expiration date set from its date of manufacture, not from the date you bought it — plastic degrades and safety standards move on over years of sun and temperature swings in a parked car. The Graco and Britax here both carry a 10-year window; the Clek expires after 9. If you're buying secondhand or a seat that's sat in a warehouse for a while, the manufacture date on the seat itself, not the purchase date, is what to check against these figures.

## Matching the specs to your car

- **You want to rear-face as long as possible** — the rear-facing weight range is the spec to optimize, keeping in mind the height limit usually binds first.
- **You'll install with LATCH only and don't plan to switch** — the LATCH weight limit matters more than the seat's overall weight range, since it's the one that actually caps how long that install method works.
- **One seat needs to last from infancy through early school age** — harness position count is what determines how much adjustment you get without buying a second seat.
- **You're buying secondhand or keeping a seat in reserve** — check the expiration date against the manufacture date stamped on the shell, not the price tag.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher LATCH weight limit make a car seat safer?**

Not on its own. Both a LATCH install and a seat belt install are federally tested and approved installation methods; the LATCH weight limit is an installation constraint, not a crash-safety rating. Once a child passes the LATCH weight cap, switching to a seat belt install is exactly as safe — it's simply the method the seat is built to use at that weight.

**If a seat is rated to 50 lb rear-facing, will my child actually rear-face until then?**

Usually not by weight alone. Convertible seats also carry a height limit — often expressed as how far a child's head sits below the shell's top edge — and most children reach that height limit well before the weight ceiling. The published weight range is one of two constraints, and the spec table can't tell you which one your particular child will hit first.

**Can I keep using a convertible car seat past its expiration date?**

Manufacturers say no, and it's not a sales tactic: the shell's plastic degrades with age and heat exposure regardless of how lightly the seat was used, and an expired seat also falls outside the window a manufacturer tracks for safety recalls. If you don't know a used seat's manufacture date, treat it as expired until you can confirm otherwise from the label molded into the shell.
