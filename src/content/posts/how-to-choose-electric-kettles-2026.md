Electric kettles look like the simplest appliance in the kitchen — it boils water, what else is there to know? In practice four published specs explain most of the real difference: how much it holds, how much power it draws, whether it can stop short of boiling, and what the body is made of. This guide explains what each changes, then lays three kettles side by side.

## Capacity sets how often you refill, not how fast it heats

The liter figure is the maximum fill line. Two of the three kettles here publish the same 1.7 L capacity — enough for three or four mugs in one pass. The Fellow is the outlier at 0.9 L, roughly half that, a deliberate trade for its gooseneck design rather than an oversight.

A bigger tank means fewer refills across a day of tea or coffee, but it's a convenience spec, not a performance one. A single morning pour-over barely needs 0.9 L and heats faster for being smaller; refilling mugs for a household all day is where the larger tank earns its size.

## Wattage sets how fast the water gets there, not how it tastes

All three sit between 1200 W and 1500 W, the normal band for a kettle on a standard outlet. Higher wattage doesn't change the water once it's hot — 212°F from a 1200 W element and 212°F from a 1500 W element are the same water — it only shortens the wait. The Fellow's lower wattage tracks its smaller tank: less water needs less power to reach the same temperature in a comparable time.

Wattage matters more than the spec sheet suggests on a shared kitchen circuit: a 1500 W kettle next to a toaster on the same breaker is a common way to trip it, and that risk scales with wattage, not with how good the kettle is.

## Temperature control is the spec that decides what you can brew

This is where the three genuinely diverge. The Hamilton Beach has no variable control at all — it heats to boiling and switches off, with boil-dry protection as its only other electronic feature. The Cosori adds five preset temperature stops, though it doesn't publish the exact degree value each one targets. The Fellow goes further, with to-the-degree control across a published 104–212°F range in 0.5°F increments, plus a Hold mode that maintains a chosen temperature for up to an hour.

That range matters for anything brewed below a full boil. Green tea is typically steeped well under 212°F, and pour-over coffee recipes often call for a specific temperature in the 195–205°F band — a boil-only kettle forces you to guess by letting it cool, or buy a separate thermometer. It isn't a worse kettle for tea or coffee that wants a full boil; it simply isn't built for recipes that call for anything less.

## Body material decides how the kettle ages, not just how it looks

Stainless steel is the common thread across all three, but what's published about it differs in ways that matter over years rather than on day one. The Fellow specifies 304 18/8 stainless steel for the body with a BPA-free plastic handle and lid pull. The Cosori specifies a plastic-free stainless steel interior specifically, which matters if plastic contact with boiling water concerns you. The Hamilton Beach states a BPA-free interior but doesn't publish what the exterior housing is made of — not evidence of a problem, but it means that axis can't be compared before buying.

## Matching the specs to your kitchen

Rather than one pick, the useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **You just want water boiled, fast, every time** — wattage and capacity are the specs to optimise; skip paying for temperature control you won't use.
- **You brew tea or coffee that calls for a temperature below boiling** — temperature control gates what you can make, and it's the axis where these three differ most.
- **You're refilling mugs across a whole household** — capacity, since the smallest kettle here holds roughly half what the other two do.
- **Plastic contact with boiling water is a concern** — body material, specifically whether the interior is published as plastic-free.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do higher-wattage kettles boil water that tastes different?**

No — wattage changes how long heating takes, not the water's chemistry once it reaches a given temperature. A 1200 W kettle and a 1500 W kettle both produce 212°F water; the difference is purely how many seconds you wait for it.

**Why does the most expensive kettle here have the smallest tank?**

Because capacity isn't what the premium tier is selling. The Fellow's 0.9 L tank is sized for pour-over brewing, and its price buys to-the-degree temperature control and a Hold mode rather than a bigger tank.

**Is a kettle with preset temperatures as precise as one with a dial?**

It depends on what the maker publishes. The Cosori offers five presets without stating the exact degree value for each, so you get a consistent stop without knowing precisely what temperature it is. A kettle that publishes an exact range and increment, like the Fellow's 104–212°F in 0.5°F steps, is the only way to confirm precision rather than just consistency.
