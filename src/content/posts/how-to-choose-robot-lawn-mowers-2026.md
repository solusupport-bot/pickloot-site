Robot lawn mowers all look like the same low disc gliding across a lawn, and most marketing copy leans on "AI" and "smart" rather than numbers. In practice, four published specs decide whether a given model finishes your yard cleanly or struggles with it: cutting width, maximum working area, navigation method, and slope handling. This guide explains what each one physically changes, then lays three current models side by side against their own spec sheets.

## Cutting width decides how many passes a lawn needs

Cutting width is the diameter of the blade disc underneath the mower, and it sets how much grass one pass removes. A wider disc covers more ground per pass, which matters most on larger lawns where the mower makes many passes per session — on a small yard, the difference between roughly 7 and 9 inches barely changes total mow time, since most robot mowers run a randomized or grid pattern rather than efficient parallel stripes anyway.

Cutting width matters most on the largest lawns: the Mammotion's dual-disc system exists because a single narrow disc would need too many passes to cover 5,000 square meters on a reasonable schedule.

## Maximum working area is a per-charge number, not a lawn-size guarantee

The working-area figure on a spec sheet describes flat, unobstructed lawn under ideal conditions — it is not a promise about your specific yard. Trees, flower beds, narrow side-yard passages and slopes all cut into how much of that rated area a mower actually finishes per charge cycle, because obstacles force extra navigation and slopes reduce coverage speed.

The tier gap here is real, not cosmetic: the Navimow i105 is rated for roughly an eighth of an acre, while the Mammotion LUBA 2 AWD 5000 is rated for over ten times that, with a published per-charge limit of 1,500 m² before it must recharge. Buying more working-area capacity than your lawn needs mostly buys fewer charge cycles per week, not a better cut.

## Navigation method determines setup effort, not mowing quality

Two approaches show up in this category. Wire-free systems use RTK-corrected satellite positioning, sometimes paired with a camera as in Segway's VisionFence or Mammotion's iNavi, to build a map with no cable in the ground. Boundary-wire systems, including the option Husqvarna still offers, use a physical wire around the mowing area that the mower detects directly.

Neither produces a visibly different cut once running correctly. The real trade-off is installation and reliability: wire-free setup skips laying cable but depends on a clear view of the sky for satellite reception, which can degrade under dense tree canopy or near tall buildings. A boundary wire takes longer to install once but doesn't care what the sky looks like.

## Slope handling is a hard limit, not a suggestion

The published slope percentage is the maximum incline the mower's wheels can climb while keeping traction — not a soft recommendation. Two-wheel-drive mowers in this category top out around 20-30%, while all-wheel-drive models like the Mammotion are rated far higher, at 80%, because power reaches all wheels instead of two.

Exceeding the rating typically shows up as wheel spin and an incomplete mowing pattern on that section, rather than the mower stalling outright. It's one of the few specs here where going past the number has an immediate physical consequence.

## Matching the specs to your yard

- **Your lawn is small and mostly open** — a compact wire-free unit rated for an eighth of an acre or so covers you with room to spare; cutting width and working area matter less here.
- **Your property has real hills** — check slope handling first. A model rated at 20-30% will underperform on grades an all-wheel-drive unit handles without issue.
- **You have dense tree cover or tall structures nearby** — navigation method is the binding constraint, since satellite/RTK wire-free systems need a clear view of the sky that a boundary-wire install doesn't.
- **Your lawn is a half-acre or larger** — working area and cutting width together, since a smaller-disc unit needs proportionally more passes and charge cycles to finish the same ground.
- **You want to avoid any cable installation** — confirm the wire-free model's stated reception requirements against how open your yard actually is before ruling out a boundary-wire option.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do robot lawn mowers work well on lawns with a lot of trees or nearby buildings?**

It depends on the navigation method more than the mower itself. A wire-free, satellite-based system can lose positioning accuracy under thick canopy or near tall structures that block its signal, while a boundary-wire system is unaffected since it senses the physical wire directly.

**Can a robot mower fully replace mowing, or is a trimmer still needed?**

Most disc-style robot mowers leave a narrow uncut strip along hard edges, fences and tight corners, because the cutting disc sits inset from the body's outer edge. A separate string trimmer or edger is still the common way to finish those strips, regardless of tier.

**What happens if part of the lawn is steeper than the mower's rated slope?**

Manufacturers generally recommend excluding that section from the mapped mowing zone rather than expecting the mower to manage it. Models with multi-zone mapping, including all three compared here, let you draw a smaller boundary that stops short of the steep section.
