Webcams look nearly identical in listing photos — a small lens on a plastic clip — and the marketing copy repeats the same handful of words across every price tier. Four published specs account for most of the real difference: how resolution trades off against frame rate, how wide a field of view the lens covers, what generation of low-light correction it runs, and how the microphone is designed. Here's what each one changes, and three models compared side by side.

## Resolution and frame rate trade off against each other

A spec sheet usually leads with top resolution, but the frame rate attached to it often drops as that number climbs. All three cameras here do 1080p, but only at 30 fps on the cheapest model, while the other two push 1080p to 60 fps or step up to 4K at 30 fps instead.

Higher resolution means more static detail, useful if you crop in or the footage is viewed at full size. A higher frame rate at a lower resolution means smoother motion, which shows when you gesture, write on a whiteboard, or share a fast-moving screen. For a typical seated call, 30 fps at 1080p is already what most meeting platforms encode anyway; 60 fps earns its keep mainly when the subject is moving.

## Field of view decides how much of the room — and how close you can sit

A 78° fixed field of view is the long-standing default: wide enough to frame head and shoulders at a normal monitor distance, with no setting to change it. Cameras that publish a range — 90°, 78°, 65° here — let you pick the angle with software instead of being stuck with whatever the lens shipped at.

Wider angles pull more of the room into frame, useful when two people share a desk or a whiteboard needs to be visible, but they also add background clutter and can mildly distort faces sitting close to the lens. A narrower angle crops tighter and flatters a single face, at the cost of cutting off anyone beside you.

## Low-light technology is a generation name, not a number you can compare across brands

"RightLight 2," "RightLight 4," and "RightLight 5" are Logitech's own version labels for the processing that brightens and balances exposure in a dim room. Within one maker's lineup, a higher number generally means more processing — HDR handling of mixed light and shadow, and in the newest version, AI enhancement aimed specifically at the face rather than the whole frame.

What the spec sheet won't tell you is how much visible difference that makes in your room. A version number shows where a camera sits in its own maker's lineup, not a measured improvement — and it isn't a figure you can line up against a different brand's low-light tech, since there's no shared published metric to compare it against.

## Microphone design determines whether you still need a separate one

Every camera here ships with two built-in microphones, but "dual mics," "dual noise-reducing mics," and "dual beamforming noise-reducing mics" describe three different levels of processing. Plain dual mics capture stereo sound with no stated filtering. Noise reduction suppresses steady background sound like a fan. Beamforming narrows the pickup pattern toward the speaker before noise reduction is applied, which matters more when there's other activity off to the side.

None of this is published as a measured decibel figure, so it can only be compared on which processing features the manufacturer states the microphone has — not on a number.

## Matching the specs to your desk

- **Your room has inconsistent or weak lighting** — weigh the low-light correction generation most; it's what changes the picture before any other setting.
- **You move, gesture, or share a live screen often** — frame rate at 1080p matters more than the headline resolution, since the top resolution usually carries the lowest frame rate.
- **You sit close to the monitor, or need to fit a second person or a whiteboard in frame** — check field of view and whether it's adjustable, rather than fixed at one angle.
- **You're on calls without a headset, or your space is noisy** — microphone design, specifically whether noise reduction or beamforming is stated.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does 4K resolution actually matter for ordinary video calls?**

Less than the spec sheet implies. Most meeting platforms downscale incoming video to 1080p or lower before sending it to other participants, regardless of what the camera captured. A 4K sensor's benefit shows up mainly in local recording or streaming, where the platform isn't compressing the feed first — not in a typical team call.

**Why would I need to adjust the field of view at all?**

Because the "right" angle depends on where you sit, not on the camera. A wide fixed angle close to a monitor can include more of your room than you want, and can mildly stretch facial features near the frame's edge. An adjustable camera lets you narrow that angle for a single-person desk, or widen it when someone joins you.

**Can a built-in microphone really replace a dedicated microphone?**

For routine calls, a noise-reducing or beamforming built-in mic is often good enough. For podcasting, recorded narration, or a consistently noisy room, a dedicated microphone still tends to outperform it — but manufacturers don't publish a comparative audio-quality number that would confirm that gap from the spec sheet alone; it's a difference you'd need to judge by ear.
