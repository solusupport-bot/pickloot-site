Blenders in this price range all look like the same idea — a jar on a motor base — but the numbers manufacturers publish diverge more than the shape suggests. Motor rating, container capacity, speed control, and jar/blade material are the specs that show up consistently, even when the units don't match brand to brand. This guide explains what each changes, then lays three models side by side.

## Motor power isn't measured the same way across brands

This is the first place the comparison gets messy. Hamilton Beach publishes a peak wattage (700 W). Ninja publishes both an operating and a peak wattage (1,200 W / 1,400 W). Vitamix publishes horsepower instead (2 HP) — a different unit, not a straight conversion of either wattage figure.

What the rating buys you is the ability to keep blade speed up against resistance — ice, frozen fruit, a thick nut butter — rather than bogging down. A low-rated motor still handles soft fruit and liquid fine; it's the harder ingredients that expose the gap. None of the three publish torque or blade-speed figures, which would be the more direct comparison — wattage and horsepower are both proxies for it.

## Container capacity is a ceiling, and the units aren't interchangeable either

Capacity ranges from 40 oz to 72 oz, but read the fine print: Ninja's 72 oz is the total pitcher size, while the maker separately publishes a 64 oz line as the maximum for liquids — the extra ounces are headroom, not usable volume. Hamilton Beach and Vitamix each publish one capacity figure with no separate liquid-max line.

A bigger container isn't automatically a better blend of a small portion; thin liquid in an oversized jar can spin past the blades without fully engaging. If you're blending for one, the smallest capacity here is arguably closer to the right size than a limitation.

## Speed control ranges from buttons to a dial, and that changes what "repeatable" means

Hamilton Beach's page describes "12 blending functions controlled by 5 simple buttons," with no numbered speed range behind them. Ninja lists 4 manual speeds plus 3 Auto-iQ programs that run a timed sequence rather than a flat speed. Vitamix publishes 10 variable speeds on a dial, plus pulse.

The practical difference is repeatability. A numbered dial lets you return to the exact same setting next time; a named button or automatic program gives consistency within that preset but less control over it. Chasing a specific texture, not just "blended," is what more granular speed control is for.

## Blade and jar material is the spec most likely to go unpublished

Of the four specs here, this one has the most gaps. Hamilton Beach names its blade material (stainless steel) and jar material (glass) directly. Ninja and Vitamix don't state what their blades are made of on the pages checked for this guide, and Vitamix doesn't state its container material either. That's not the same as a bad spec — it just means you can't compare on this axis for two of the three without a manual or a third-party teardown.

One practical note regardless of brand: a glass jar won't scratch, stain, or pick up odors the way some plastics can over years of use, but it also weighs more and is less forgiving if dropped.

## Matching the specs to your kitchen

Rather than one pick, the more useful question is which constraint actually binds for you.

- **You blend single servings most days** — capacity matters less than you'd think; even the smallest jar here is oversized for one smoothie, so motor consistency and speed control matter more.
- **You batch-cook for a household** — check capacity first, and read closely which number applies to what you're actually making (a total pitcher size versus a stated liquid-max line).
- **You regularly blend ice, frozen fruit, or nuts** — motor rating is what's being tested, even though the units here don't convert cleanly between brands.
- **You want a repeatable result, not just "blended"** — a numbered speed dial lets you return to the exact setting next time; a named button or automatic program doesn't.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does Vitamix list horsepower instead of watts?**

Vitamix's own spec sheet states motor power in horsepower (2 HP for the E310), with no wattage figure alongside it. Horsepower and wattage measure a motor differently, and there's no simple conversion from one into a number comparable to a wattage-rated blender — treat them as separate scales.

**Does a higher wattage always mean a stronger blend?**

Not reliably by itself. Wattage or horsepower shows how much power the motor can draw, but blending performance also depends on blade design, jar shape, and how the control electronics manage speed under load — none of which shows up in a single power figure. It's a useful proxy, not a complete answer.

**Why don't all three list what the blades are made of?**

Manufacturer pages vary in how much detail they publish. Hamilton Beach states its blades are stainless steel; the Ninja and Vitamix pages checked for this guide didn't state a blade material at all. An unpublished spec isn't evidence the part is lower quality — comparing on that point would just take a manual or a teardown, not this page.
