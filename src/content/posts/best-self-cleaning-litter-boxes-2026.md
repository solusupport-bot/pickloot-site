Every self-cleaning litter box makes the same promise: you stop scooping. The photos do not show that they work in different ways, and four published specs account for most of the difference: the litter they accept, how much waste they hold, the weight range their sensors cover, and the size of the opening.

## Litter type decides what you keep reordering

This is the first fork in the category. Tray-based systems run on the manufacturer's own consumable: the PetSafe unit here is published as taking ScoopFree crystal litter in a disposable lined tray, so litter and container are a single item you reorder from one supplier. Rake-and-sift units take litter off the shelf — PETKIT publishes compatibility with "99% of clumping litters," listing clay, tofu, corn and bentonite, while Whisker recommends a "standard-weight, clay-based clumping litter" for the Litter-Robot 4.

This is a question about supply, not performance. If you already buy clumping clay, the Litter-Robot's recommendation costs you nothing; if you have moved to tofu or corn, it narrows your choice, and the crystal system removes the choice entirely. It stops mattering if your cat has only ever used one litter.

## Waste capacity is a measure of how often you are involved

The three units publish this in three different units of measurement. PETKIT publishes a volume: a 7 L waste bin. Whisker publishes a duration instead — "up to 8 days before emptying the waste drawer in a 1-cat household" — and no volume figure. PetSafe publishes neither for the Classic.

A volume you can check against your own household. A days figure already contains an assumption about how many cats are using it, and that assumption is one cat, so two cats roughly halve it. Neither number helps if the box sits somewhere you never walk past, which is where the app column earns its place: both connected units report waste level, so you find out before the drawer is full rather than after.

## Cat weight range is a sensor spec, not a size limit

Both connected units publish a range — 3.3-22 lb for the PETKIT, 3-25 lb for the Litter-Robot 4 — and the useful half is the bottom end. These boxes detect an occupant by weight and hold the cleaning cycle until the cat leaves, so a cat lighter than the published minimum is one the unit may not register. That is a kitten problem far more than an adult-cat problem.

The upper figure is about structure and sensor calibration, not whether a large cat physically fits. The PetSafe Classic publishes no range at all: it is documented as having safety sensors that hold the rake while a cat is present, but with no stated window there is nothing to check a kitten against.

## Entry dimensions decide which cats will actually use it

An automatic box a cat refuses is not a self-cleaning box. The Litter-Robot 4 publishes a 15.75 x 15.75 in opening, a 6.9 in step height and 16.5 in of interior height; PETKIT publishes an 8.03 in entrance height; the PetSafe Classic publishes no entry dimensions at all.

Step height matters for a senior or arthritic cat, and for a kitten. Opening width matters for a large cat that needs to turn around inside rather than back out. Interior height matters if your cat stands rather than crouches — a low ceiling is a common reason a cat uses a box once and never again. A young cat of average size clears all of these without noticing them.

## Matching the specs to your home

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **No consumable tied to one supplier** — read litter compatibility, which separates the three cleanly.
- **The box lives somewhere you rarely walk past** — waste capacity plus app waste-level reporting.
- **A kitten or a senior cat** — the published lower weight bound, then the step height.
- **A large cat, or more than two cats** — the opening and interior dimensions first, then capacity.
- **Replacing a box your cat already refuses** — entry dimensions only. Nothing else matters if they will not step in.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do self-cleaning litter boxes cost more to run than a regular box?**

It depends on the litter column. A tray-based system carries a proprietary consumable you reorder for the life of the unit, so its running cost is structural. Units that take ordinary clumping litter run closer to a standard box.

**Will my cat actually use an automatic litter box?**

A cautious cat usually needs the transition managed rather than forced. Keeping the old box available is standard advice, and several units can run on a manual or delayed cycle at first so nothing moves while the cat is inside. The entry dimensions above are the specs most likely to predict a flat refusal.

**How many cats can share one self-cleaning litter box?**

That is a waste-capacity question, and the published figures make it harder to answer than it should be. A stated days-per-empty figure assumes a single cat, so two or three cats divide it accordingly, while a published bin volume you can reason about directly. Where nothing is published, there is nothing to divide.
