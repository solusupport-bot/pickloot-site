Noise-cancelling headphones are sold on adjectives - immersive, adaptive, world-class - and adjectives are not comparable between brands. Published figures are. Five of them account for most of what you notice on a long flight: battery life with cancellation running, weight, driver diameter, which codecs the maker lists, and full recharge time.

## Battery life is quoted with ANC on for a reason

Active cancellation runs a microphone array and a processor continuously, so it draws power whether or not you play audio. That is why makers publish two numbers, and why only the ANC-on figure is useful for travel planning. One over-ear model here publishes 30 hours with cancellation on and 40 hours with it off.

This stops mattering once the number comfortably exceeds your longest trip. Thirty and forty hours both exceed any single flight, so above roughly a day of playback the spec becomes a question of how often you want to think about charging. It matters far more at the other end of the scale: an earbud rated for six hours relies on the case, not the bud, to carry a long-haul flight, so read the number of case charges alongside the per-charge hours.

## Weight is the spec you feel after hour three

Over-ear headphones in this class cluster between roughly 230 g and 260 g, and inside that band clamping force and pad shape matter more than mass. Twenty grams is about four sheets of paper.

Weight becomes decisive only across form factors. Earbuds are an order of magnitude lighter on your head, and the weight moves to a case in your bag. What you are choosing between is not really grams but where the pressure sits: a headband and pads around the ears, or a tip inside the ear canal. People who find one intolerable often find the other fine, so this axis is worth settling first.

## Driver diameter describes the moving part, not the sound

The driver is the diaphragm that moves air. A larger one displaces more air per excursion, which is why bigger drivers are generally easier to get low-frequency output from - a 40 mm driver has about 1.8 times the area of a 30 mm one.

It does not follow that a larger driver sounds better. Tuning, enclosure design and the DSP applied on top shape the output more than diameter does, and none of that appears in a spec sheet. Read driver size as a fact about the hardware, not a prediction about the result. Not every maker publishes it - in-ear designs often do not - so this axis sometimes cannot be compared.

## Published codec support sets the ceiling on the wireless link

A codec is the compression scheme used over Bluetooth, and both ends have to support the same one. SBC and AAC are the baseline every device speaks. Beyond that, LDAC carries a higher bitrate and is mainly an Android feature; LC3 is used by Bluetooth LE Audio; aptX Adaptive is a Qualcomm scheme requiring a Snapdragon Sound phone at the other end.

The practical consequence is narrower than the marketing suggests. If your phone only supports AAC, a headset's LDAC support does nothing for you. Read a codec list against your own phone rather than as a quality score - and where a maker publishes a partial list, the rest is unknown, not absent. Charge time is the least contested of the five: two to three and a half hours across all three.

## Matching the specs to your flight

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **You do not want to think about charging on a long-haul trip** - the ANC-on battery figure, where over-ear designs are measured in tens of hours.
- **Cabin bag space is tight, or a headband bothers you** - form factor first, then the case-charge count rather than the per-charge hours.
- **You stream from an Android phone and care about the link** - the published codec list, checked against what your phone supports.
- **You wear them a full working day, not just a flight** - weight, and the fact that clamping force is never published, so this axis is worth trying in person.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does noise cancellation work equally well on all types of noise?**

No. Active cancellation is most effective against constant low-frequency sound - engine drone, air conditioning hum - because the system can predict a repeating waveform and generate its inverse. It is far less effective on sudden or high-frequency sounds like a crying baby, and that limit applies to every design in the category rather than to any one model.

**Can I use these for phone calls, or just listening?**

All three have microphones for calls. Call quality depends on the microphone array and the voice-processing tuning, and none of these makers publishes a comparable figure for either, so it is not an axis you can settle from spec sheets.

**Is it safe to wear noise-cancelling headphones for an entire long-haul flight?**

The usual guidance for any headphone is to take a break every couple of hours, to rest your ears and stay aware of announcements. Cancellation works by adding an inverted signal rather than by increasing pressure, though it does reduce awareness of your surroundings - a consideration on the ground rather than in a seat.
