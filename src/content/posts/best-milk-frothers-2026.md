On a shelf, milk frothers look like the same object in three sizes: a jug, a lid and a whisk. The published specifications separate them more than the marketing does - how much milk each one will froth, whether cold foam is a setting at all, which parts survive a dishwasher and how much counter the base occupies. This guide explains what each of those figures changes, then lays three models out side by side.

## Froth capacity and hot-milk capacity are two different numbers

Most frothers publish two maximum fill levels, and the gap is large. Foam needs headroom to expand into, so the frothing line sits well below the heating line - Nespresso publishes 120 ml for milk froth against 240 ml for hot milk, and Instant 8.5 oz for frothing against 17 oz for heating. In both cases frothing caps out at half the heating figure.

This matters because retail listings usually quote the larger number. If you are making foamed drinks, the froth line caps how many you get per run, and it is the figure to compare. Breville publishes a single 500 ml maximum fill marking for its cappuccino and latte discs and no separate hot-milk figure, a reminder that both specs are not always on the page.

Where it stops mattering: if you make one drink at a time, every capacity here clears it comfortably.

## Settings decide whether cold foam is possible at all

"Settings" means two different things here. Instant and Nespresso work by preset buttons - three functions on the Aeroccino3 (hot froth, cold froth, hot milk), four on the Instant unit, which splits foam density into light and dense as a separate axis from temperature. Breville instead ships two frothing discs, described in its manual as a cappuccino disc for thick froth and a latte disc for smooth milk, plus a cold stir cycle that runs 60 seconds and stops.

The practical question is whether cold foam appears on the spec sheet at all, since it is not something you can improvise on a machine built only to heat. If you only ever make hot lattes, the difference between three presets and four is close to irrelevant.

## Dishwasher-safe parts are the spec you feel daily

Milk protein sets onto a warm surface quickly, so cleanup is not a footnote here, and the three units publish three different answers. Instant lists the lid and whisk as dishwasher-safe and the powered base as not. Breville lists the jug, jug lid, measuring cap and frothing discs as top-shelf dishwasher-safe, while recommending hand washing the discs to extend their life. Nespresso publishes no dishwasher-safe parts for the Aeroccino3 and specifies rinsing with warm or hot water instead.

If you froth once or twice a week, rinsing is a 20-second job. If you froth every morning, this column decides whether the appliance stays on the counter.

## Footprint is set by the base, not the jug

Height is the dimension that causes problems, because frothers usually live under a wall cabinet and the lid has to come off. Breville publishes 6.4 x 7.5 x 10.4 in, Instant 4.7 x 6.2 x 8.8 in, and the Aeroccino3 a 9 cm cylinder standing 17 cm tall - roughly 3.5 in across and 6.7 in high. That is a real difference in counter planning, and it does not track the price band.

## Matching the specs to your kitchen

Rather than one pick, the useful question is which constraint binds hardest for you.

- **You make one drink at a time** - froth capacity is not the binding spec; settings and cleaning are.
- **You make drinks for two or more back to back** - the froth line rather than the jug volume, since that caps a single run.
- **Iced coffee is part of the routine** - the settings column decides it; a cold setting is either published or it is not.
- **The counter sits under a wall cabinet** - the height figure, and lid clearance in place.
- **You froth most days** - dishwasher-safe parts, the friction you repeat.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the type of milk change which specs matter?**

Oat and almond milk have less protein and fat than dairy to stabilise foam, so they generally froth less consistently unless you use a barista-formulated version. None of the three manufacturers publishes separate capacity or timing figures for non-dairy milk, so the spec sheets will not settle this axis - the settings column is the closest proxy, since more foam-density options give you more to adjust.

**Can these be used for hot chocolate, or only coffee drinks?**

They heat and froth milk, so hot chocolate works the same way as a latte base. The relevant spec is the hot-milk capacity rather than the froth capacity, since you are heating rather than foaming - which is where the Breville's unpublished hot-milk figure is a genuine gap in the comparison.

**How often do these need cleaning or descaling?**

All three manuals specify cleaning after each use; the Aeroccino3 is rinse-only, while the other two have dishwasher-safe parts. Descaling applies to units whose heating element meets water deposits over time, and the published guidance is to follow the interval in each manual rather than a fixed calendar - hard water shortens it.
