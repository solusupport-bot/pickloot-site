Electric toothbrushes are marketed on numbers that sound decisive and mostly are not — two of the three handles below publish the same movements-per-minute figure despite sitting at opposite ends of the range. What actually varies is the mechanical action of the head, how many modes the handle offers, how the pressure sensor reports back, and whether any of it reaches a phone.

## Cleaning action is the one mechanical difference

This is the split that matters most and gets discussed least. Sonic handles hold a brush-shaped head and vibrate it side to side at high frequency. Oscillation-rotation handles hold a small round head that rotates back and forth, with micro-vibrations layered on top.

The practical difference is how you use them. A round oscillating head is held against one tooth at a time and moved along the arch, because the head does the scrubbing motion for you. A sonic head is swept along the gumline more like a manual brush, and its motion drives fluid between the teeth rather than reaching there directly. Neither action is a grade of the other; both are long-established designs. What it does mean is that switching between them requires changing your technique, and heads never cross over.

## Brushing modes matter only if you will change them

The modes run from one to five here. A single-mode handle does one thing when you press the button. Three modes typically add a lower-intensity sensitive setting and a longer whitening cycle. Five modes plus three intensities gives you a fifteen-way grid.

The spec stops mattering almost immediately for most people, because handles get used on one setting. The exceptions are worth knowing: a sensitive mode helps if brushing currently causes discomfort or you have recently had dental work. If that does not apply, additional modes are buttons you will press once. The premium handle approaches it differently — rather than a sensitive mode you select, its sensor lowers intensity on its own when it detects too much pressure.

## Movements per minute is the spec that separates nothing

Both Philips handles publish 62,000 brush movements per minute. One sits at the entry of the range and one at the top. Oral-B does not publish a figure for the iO Series 3 at all, because an oscillating head's motion is not comparable to a vibrating one anyway — rotations and vibrations are different units of the same marketing idea.

The useful conclusion is to stop reading this number as a quality tier. Within one manufacturer's sonic line the frequency is largely constant across price points, so where it is cited as the reason a handle costs more, it is not doing that work.

## Pressure sensor feedback is the spec worth paying attention to

All three handles detect excessive pressure; what differs is how they tell you. The entry handle vibrates in your hand. The mid handle lights a ring that distinguishes too little, correct, and too much. The premium handle shows a visual signal and reduces the motor's intensity by itself.

This matters because brushing too hard contributes to gum recession and enamel wear over time, and it is difficult to self-assess — the pressure that feels thorough is usually the pressure that is excessive. A three-state light tells you where the acceptable band is, where an on-off buzz only tells you when you have left it. If you have ever been told you brush hard, this is the axis to compare on.

## Matching the specs to your bathroom

- **Someone has flagged your brushing pressure** — the sensor's feedback type, since a graded indicator teaches the range and a buzz only flags the breach.
- **Brushing is uncomfortable right now** — a sensitive mode, or the automatic intensity reduction on the premium handle.
- **You travel often** — operating time, with the caveat that only one of these three publishes one, so this may come down to asking the manufacturer directly.
- **You dislike the feel of a brush-shaped head** — cleaning action, because the round oscillating head is a different object entirely and the heads are not interchangeable.
- **You want to see coverage data** — app connection, which only the premium handle has.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I really need a pressure sensor?**

It is among the more evidence-supported features in the category rather than a convenience add-on, because brushing too hard is a common cause of gum recession and enamel wear, and it is hard to notice without an external cue. All three handles here include one, so the question is not whether to have it but which style of feedback you will actually respond to.

**Why is battery life missing from the spec table?**

Because two of these three manufacturers do not publish it for these models. Philips states up to two weeks for the ProtectiveClean 4100; neither Oral-B for the iO Series 3 nor Philips for the 9900 Prestige publishes an operating-time figure that can be compared like for like. Rather than fill those cells with estimates, the table leaves the spec out.

**How often should the brush head be replaced?**

Manufacturers and dental associations generally advise replacing it every three months, or sooner if the bristles are visibly splayed. This applies across all three handles, so it is worth checking head availability before committing to a model — sonic and oscillating heads do not cross over.
