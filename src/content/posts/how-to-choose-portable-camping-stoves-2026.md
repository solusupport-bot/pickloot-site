Portable stoves all look like variations on the same small burner, but three published numbers account for most of the real difference: burner output, boil time, and weight. A fourth, ignition type, is about convenience rather than performance. None of these numbers mean quite what they look like at first glance — a higher output rating doesn't guarantee a faster boil, and the lightest stove on paper isn't always the lightest kit you'll actually carry.

## Burner output doesn't predict boil speed on its own

BTU per hour (or watts) measures how much heat the burner can generate, not how efficiently that heat reaches the water in the pot. The Jetboil Flash is rated at 9,000 BTU/h yet boils half a liter in 100 seconds, because its FluxRing heat exchanger wraps more of the flame around the cooking vessel instead of letting it escape around the sides. The Soto WindMaster is rated higher, at 11,000 BTU/h, but takes almost 2.5 minutes to do a comparable job with a conventional pot that has no heat exchanger. MSR doesn't publish an output figure for the PocketRocket 2 at all — you only get its boil time, also with a plain pot.

The takeaway: a BTU or watt number only tells you something when you're comparing stoves that pair with the same style of pot. Across pot designs, it tells you almost nothing about which one will actually boil water faster.

## Boil time only means what the test conditions mean

None of these three boil-time figures were measured the same way. MSR tests 1 liter with its own IsoPro fuel. Soto tests 2 cups — roughly 0.47 liters — specifically in wind. Jetboil tests half a liter and averages the result over the full life of a canister as it loses pressure near the end. "100 seconds," "3.5 minutes," and "under 2.5 minutes" are not three points on the same scale; they're three different experiments with different volumes and different conditions.

Before treating any boil time as a ranking, check what volume it was measured against and whether wind, altitude, or canister age were part of the test. A faster-sounding number measured under easier conditions isn't actually faster.

## Weight is a real tradeoff, not a universal win

Bare-burner stoves like the PocketRocket 2 and WindMaster weigh under 3 ounces because that figure covers only the burner head and valve — you supply your own pot, lid, and windscreen separately. The Flash's 13.1-ounce weight looks heavier by comparison, but that number already includes the pot, insulating cozy, and fuel-canister stand, which are items a bare-burner setup would need you to add back from somewhere else.

So the smallest number on the spec sheet isn't necessarily the lightest kit once everything is accounted for. If you're optimizing pack weight, compare total system weight — stove plus pot plus support — rather than the stove's standalone figure.

## Ignition type is a convenience, not a performance spec

A built-in igniter saves you from carrying a lighter, but none of these three manufacturers warranty their igniter against failure in cold or at altitude, where piezo-style ignition is known to get unreliable. MSR doesn't include one on the PocketRocket 2 at all — that's a genuine cost and weight saving, not an oversight, and MSR sells a separate piezo accessory for exactly this gap. Soto's built-in "Stealth igniter" and Jetboil's push-button are both integrated into the stove body, but an igniter is still a mechanical part that can wear out well before the burner does.

Treat the ignition column as "do I need to also pack a lighter," not as a deciding factor — the practical advice from every stove's own manual is to carry backup ignition regardless.

## Matching the specs to your trip

- **Pack weight is the tightest constraint** — weight is the spec to optimize, but add back the cost of a pot, lid, and stand before comparing a bare burner to an integrated system.
- **You regularly cook in wind or cold** — burner output and the shape of the burner head matter together; a higher quoted BTU figure means less than how well the flame is shielded.
- **You mostly boil water for coffee or freeze-dried meals** — an integrated system's boil time is the figure to read, since that's exactly the use case it was designed and measured for.
- **You don't want to carry a separate lighter** — ignition type is worth checking, though every manufacturer still recommends packing backup ignition anyway.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use any gas canister with any of these stoves?**

Not always. The Soto WindMaster's regulator is explicitly not rated for 100% propane and is designed for butane/isobutane/propane blends only, while MSR and Jetboil each specify their own branded canister — IsoPro and JetPower, respectively — for the performance figures they publish. A different brand's blend canister will usually thread on and run, but the manufacturer's boil-time and burn-duration numbers assume their own fuel.

**Why doesn't the lightest stove here have an igniter?**

Because a built-in piezo or push-button igniter adds a mechanical part that can fail, and removing it is one of the simplest ways to cut weight and cost from an ultralight design. MSR sells a separate piezo igniter accessory specifically so PocketRocket 2 owners can add that convenience back without the stove carrying the part — and its weight — by default.

**Do these stoves perform the same at altitude or in freezing temperatures?**

No manufacturer here publishes a cold-weather or altitude-adjusted figure, so the boil times on this page are all measured under moderate conditions. Canister stoves lose pressure as the gas inside cools or as altitude increases, which quietly derates every number above. Keeping a canister warm — inside a jacket pocket, never directly next to the flame — is the practical workaround with any of the three.
