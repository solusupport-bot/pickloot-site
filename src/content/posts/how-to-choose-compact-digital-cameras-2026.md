Compact digital cameras are having a real moment again — CIPA shipment data shows built-in-lens camera shipments running well ahead of last year's pace through early 2026, most of it in small, fixed-lens, zoom-equipped bodies rather than professional gear. On a shelf they can look interchangeable: a lens, a shutter button, a screen. Four published specs actually account for most of the difference: sensor size, optical zoom, video resolution, and battery life.

## Sensor size matters more than the megapixel number

Megapixel counts get top billing on packaging, but they mostly determine how large you can print or crop a photo — not how good it looks in dim light. That comes down to the physical size of the sensor itself. A larger sensor spreads the same megapixel count across bigger individual photosites, so each one gathers more light before the image turns noisy. The budget and mid-range models here both use a 1/2.3-inch sensor, a size shared by most phone cameras. The premium model's 1.0-type sensor is roughly four times the surface area, which is the main reason it costs more — not its megapixel count, which is actually lower.

## Optical zoom is a physical trade-off, not just a bigger number

A longer zoom range means a longer lens barrel that has to telescope in and out, and manufacturers who want to keep a camera pocketable generally pair a long zoom with a smaller sensor rather than a larger one — which is exactly the pattern in this table. The 40x zoom gives genuine reach a fixed 8x lens can't match, but the maximum aperture typically narrows as you zoom in, meaning less light reaches the sensor at full telephoto exactly when you need it most. A shorter, brighter zoom and a longer, dimmer one are solving different problems, not competing on the same scale.

## 4K video resolution doesn't guarantee smooth or long footage

"4K at 30p" describes pixel count and frame rate, not the bitrate, codec, or how long a camera can record before it needs to pause. Two cameras can both say 4K and produce noticeably different footage once you look at the data rate behind it — something neither manufacturer page here publishes. Frame rate is worth checking separately from resolution: 30p is standard for ordinary clips, while higher frame rates (not listed on either page above) matter for slow motion or smoother motion in fast scenes.

## Battery life (CIPA) is a lab number, not a real-world one

The CIPA rating is a standardized test — a fixed cycle of shots with the flash firing part of the time, at set intervals — designed so camera batteries can be compared on equal footing, not a prediction of how long your particular battery will last. Video recording, continuous zooming, and cold weather all drain a battery faster than the CIPA cycle simulates. A camera with no published CIPA number, like the mid-range model here, isn't necessarily worse on battery life — it's just a spec its manufacturer chose not to publish, and the "Not published" cell is honest about that gap rather than a guess filling it in.

## Matching the specs to your shooting style

- **You mostly shoot indoors or at dusk** — sensor size is the spec to prioritize, since it governs how clean an image stays as light drops.
- **Your subject is usually far away** — optical zoom range matters most, accepting the lower-light trade-off that comes with it.
- **Video is the main use case** — check video resolution and frame rate together, and look past the manufacturer page for independently measured bitrate if it isn't published.
- **The camera needs to last a full day out** — battery life matters most, and a published CIPA figure is worth more here than a slightly larger zoom range.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a bigger sensor always mean a bigger camera?**

Generally yes, within a fixed-lens compact — the lens has to be built to cover the sensor, and a larger sensor needs a larger lens opening to match. That's part of why the premium 1.0-type sensor camera here has a shorter zoom range than the smaller-sensor mid-range model: fitting a bright, fast lens over a bigger sensor into a similarly pocketable body means giving up some zoom reach.

**Is optical zoom the same as the "80x ZoomPlus" or similar figures some cameras list?**

No. Optical zoom is what the lens itself can do by moving glass elements; a second, larger number alongside it is almost always digital zoom, which crops and upscales the image rather than actually magnifying it before the sensor captures it. The spec table above lists optical zoom only, since that's the figure that reflects what the lens physically does.

**Why would I buy a dedicated camera when my phone already has multiple lenses?**

The specs above are the answer: a phone's small sensor and fixed, short focal lengths cap how much true optical zoom and low-light sensor area it can offer in a slim body. A dedicated compact trades pocket space for a bigger sensor, a longer optical zoom range, or both — which is also the reason shipment data shows this category growing again rather than shrinking.
