Portable SSDs are sold on one number — a megabytes-per-second figure on the box — and reaching it depends on a port most people have never checked. Four published specifications do the real work: the rated sequential read and write speeds, the USB interface those speeds require, the drop and ingress ratings, and the warranty term.

## Rated speeds are a ceiling, not a throughput

The figures in the table are sequential speeds: large, contiguous files moving as one stream, which is what a video file or a disk image looks like to a drive. They are measured under favourable conditions and describe a ceiling. Copy a folder of thousands of small files instead and you have handed the drive a random-access workload, which runs well below the rated figure on any of them.

Read and write are not always both published. Samsung publishes both for the T7 Shield and the T9; Crucial publishes an up-to-1,050 MB/s read figure for the X9 and no sequential write figure at all. An unpublished number is not evidence of a slow one, but it does mean the drive cannot be compared on that axis before you own it.

Where it stops mattering: over a day of backups and documents, the gap between 1,050 and 2,000 MB/s is a handful of seconds. It becomes real when you move hundreds of gigabytes, or edit video off the drive.

## The interface sets the ceiling, and your computer sets the interface

This is the spec most often missed. USB 3.2 Gen 2 runs at 10 Gbps, which caps the X9 and the T7 Shield at their published 1,050 MB/s. The T9's 2,000 MB/s needs USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 at 20 Gbps — a different, less common host port.

Gen 2x2 was never widely adopted: it is absent from Apple silicon Macs, and plenty of Windows laptops skip it too. So the port on your machine, not the drive, decides whether the T9's headline figure is reachable at all — check that specification first.

## Drop and ingress ratings describe how drives actually die

External drives fail from their environment far more often than from wear, which is what these two ratings describe — and they are separate things. A drop rating is a free-fall test; an IP rating is about dust and water.

The wording repays close reading. Crucial publishes the X9 as drop proof to 7.5 feet *onto carpeted flooring* — a real qualifier, since a tiled floor is not the tested surface. Samsung publishes 9.8 feet for both the T7 Shield and the T9. Only the T7 Shield carries a published IP rating, IP65, covering dust ingress and low-pressure water jets from any direction; the T9 publishes no IP figure.

On a desk, none of this matters. In a bag that gets rained on, the ingress rating is what you are buying.

## Warranty length is the spec that outlasts the drive

Three years is the standard term here, and Samsung's five on the T9 is the outlier. A flash warranty is usually bounded twice: Crucial's three years on the X9 runs to whichever comes first, the date or the drive's rated total bytes written. That endurance limit is generous next to ordinary use, but it is why a term is not simply a measure of confidence.

A warranty replaces hardware, never data: whatever the term, a drive holding the only copy of something is a single point of failure.

## Matching the specs to your desk and your bag

- **The drive never leaves the desk** — interface and speed are the only specs doing anything; drop and ingress ratings pay for a risk you do not have.
- **It lives in a backpack or camera bag** — the IP rating first, then the drop height, and read the surface the drop test names.
- **You edit video off the drive** — sequential write as well as read, and whether a write figure is published at all.
- **It is an archive, not a scratch disk** — warranty term, and the total-bytes-written limit inside it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I actually need a USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 port to use the T9?**

No — the drive is backwards compatible and works on an ordinary USB-C port. What you lose is the headline speed: without a Gen 2x2 host it runs at Gen 2 rates, the same ceiling as the T7 Shield and the X9. Since Apple silicon Macs and many Windows laptops lack Gen 2x2, check the port before choosing the T9 for its speed rating.

**Is an IP65 rating worth paying for?**

It depends on where the drive lives. Dust and light water exposure are ordinary, undramatic ways an external drive dies, and IP65 is a tested figure rather than a marketing adjective — so for a drive that travels it separates these tiers more than speed does. For one that sits beside a monitor, it does nothing.

**Can a portable SSD be used as a games library or a boot drive?**

Running a game library from one over USB works; load times sit between an internal NVMe drive and a mechanical external disk. Booting an operating system from an external drive is supported on some systems and not others, and it is the use that pushes total bytes written up fastest — the limit attached to the warranty term.
