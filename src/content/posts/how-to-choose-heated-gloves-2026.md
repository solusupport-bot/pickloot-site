Heated gloves all promise the same thing — warm hands, a battery pack, a few heat settings — and the product photos make them look interchangeable. What separates one pair from another is a short list of published specs: battery capacity, how many heat settings exist and what degree they're rated at, how long the battery lasts on the setting you'll use, and whether the fingertips work on a touchscreen. This guide explains what each spec changes, then lays three models side by side.

## Battery capacity tells you energy, not warmth

A mAh or Wh figure describes how much energy the battery holds, not how hot the gloves get. Two gloves with the same stated capacity can still run at different temperatures and runtimes, because the heating element's power draw — not the battery's size — sets how fast that energy gets spent. Capacity is comparable between gloves that publish it, but it can't be compared against a glove that doesn't: one of the three models here names only "a REDLITHIUM USB Battery Pack," with no mAh or Wh figure on the product page.

Read a capacity number as "how much fuel is in the tank," and look to the runtime figure for how long that fuel lasts on the setting you plan to use.

## Heat settings and the temperature behind them are two separate claims

All three gloves here advertise three heat settings, controlled by a single button or touch panel. That count tells you almost nothing on its own — "3 settings" could mean 105°F to 145°F, or an unlabeled Low/Medium/High with no degree figures published anywhere. Only one of the three models here states actual temperatures for each level; the other two describe heat only by name.

An unpublished temperature isn't evidence the glove runs cooler — it just means the ceiling can't be verified before buying, and you're relying on a heat-level name instead of a number.

## Low-setting runtime is the number to compare, not the headline "hours"

Battery-heated gear is usually marketed with a range, like "up to 8.5 hours," and that headline number is almost always the Low setting, because Low draws the least current. Runtime drops as you raise the heat — more current through the heating element empties the same battery faster. A glove that claims a long runtime on Low can still be a short-runtime glove on High.

Not every brand breaks this down by setting. One of the three gloves here publishes only a single combined "up to 6 hours" figure across all settings, rather than a per-setting breakdown, which makes it harder to know what you'd actually get on High.

## Touchscreen-compatible fingertips are a convenience spec, not a warmth spec

Touchscreen compatibility comes from a small patch of conductive material built into the thumb and index fingertip, letting a capacitive screen register touch through the glove. It has no effect on the heating element, battery, or runtime — it's a separate manufacturing decision layered onto the same glove. Two of the three models here publish this as a feature; the third's own spec sheet doesn't mention it either way, even though outside reviews describe touchscreen-friendly fingertips.

## Matching the specs to your cold-weather day

- **You're mostly stationary outdoors** (dog walking, waiting on transit, standing at an event) — low-setting runtime is the spec to optimize, since you'll likely run the glove on Low or Medium for long stretches.
- **You keep using a touchscreen device with gloves on** — touchscreen-compatible fingertips matter more than any heat figure, and it's worth confirming the spec is actually published, not just assumed from reviews.
- **Your hands run genuinely cold** — max heat level is the spec to check, though it's only a usable comparison point when the manufacturer publishes a number.
- **You want to budget a battery before buying** — battery capacity lets you do that math, and it's worth noting when a listing skips it entirely.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher mAh rating mean warmer gloves?**

No. mAh and Wh measure how much energy the battery stores, which determines runtime, not how hot the heating element gets. Maximum temperature is set by the heating element and the glove's control circuitry — a separate spec from battery capacity, and one that isn't always published alongside it.

**Why don't all heated gloves publish a maximum temperature?**

Brands vary in how much they disclose. Some state exact degrees for each heat level; others name only the levels (Low/Medium/High) with no figures attached. An unpublished temperature doesn't mean the glove runs cooler — it means that axis can't be verified before purchase, which is why the spec table marks it "Not published" rather than guessing at a number.

**How do I know if heated gloves will fit under a jacket cuff or still let me grip things?**

None of these published specs answer that. Fit and dexterity depend on the glove's cut, palm material, and whether it's a liner, a mitten, or a full glove — details that vary by size and model rather than by battery or heat specs. Checking the manufacturer's own size chart and glove type is the more reliable path than comparing numbers.
