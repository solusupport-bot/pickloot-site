E-readers look like one product with different logos on it, but four published specs account for most of what actually changes day to day: screen size, pixel density, the battery-life rating, and whether the device is rated against water. This guide explains what each one changes in practice, then lays three current models side by side so you can match the numbers to how and where you actually read.

## Screen size changes page density, not sharpness

A bigger screen does not make text crisper — that is the resolution's job — it changes how much text fits on a page before a page turn, and how the device feels to hold one-handed. The jump from 6 inches to 7 inches shows up most in formats with more visual structure, like image-heavy nonfiction, comics, or PDFs with fixed layouts that an e-reader can't reflow. For plain-text fiction, the difference is smaller than the inch figure suggests, because most readers set a font size that produces a similar number of words per screen regardless of the physical display.

## Pixel density is why text looks printed, and why color backs off

All three devices here publish 300 ppi for black-and-white text, which is the resolution band where individual pixels stop being visible at normal reading distance — this is the number that makes e-ink look like a printed page rather than a screen. Color is a different physical layer laid over that same panel, and every current color e-ink display drops to roughly half the pixel density in color mode. That is not a manufacturing shortcut on one brand; it is how the color filter layer works across the category. It matters for photos and comic panels, and barely registers for reading black text.

## The battery-life rating describes a specific test, not your experience

A "weeks of battery life" figure is run under an announced set of conditions — typically a fixed daily reading time with Wi-Fi and Bluetooth off and the front light at a moderate brightness — and manufacturers vary how they state that test. The ratings compared here (6, 12, and 8 weeks) all come from each maker's own published figure, not a converted or estimated number. Leaving wireless on for syncing, cranking the front light up, or reading for longer sessions than the test assumes will pull the real-world number below the published one on any device, so treat the gap between models as more reliable than the absolute figure on any one of them.

## Water resistance is a pass/fail rating, not a spectrum

IPX8 means a device has been tested to survive full immersion — 2 meters of fresh water for 60 minutes is the figure both water-rated models here publish — not that it merely resists splashes. A device with no published IPX rating hasn't necessarily failed that test; more often the manufacturer simply didn't build or certify the seals for it, which is also why it is typically the cheapest model in a lineup. The practical read: a rated device can go in the bath or by the pool without a case, and an unrated one should stay away from any open liquid.

## Matching the specs to how you actually read

- **You mainly read fiction and want the lowest cost** — screen size and water resistance matter least; put the weight on pixel density, which all three already clear at 300 ppi for text.
- **You read in the bath, by the pool, or somewhere a drink could spill** — water resistance is the spec to check first, since it is a pass/fail rating rather than a nice-to-have.
- **Your library is heavy on comics, manga, or illustrated cookbooks** — color pixel density is the number to compare, and expect it to run lower than the black-and-white figure on the same device.
- **You hate charging cables** — the battery-life rating is the axis to optimize, keeping in mind it was measured under the manufacturer's stated test conditions, not yours.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a bigger screen mean a heavier device?**

Generally yes, within this category — a 7-inch panel adds bulk and weight over a 6-inch one, since more glass and more battery capacity to drive it both add mass. The published dimensions and weight for any model are worth checking directly on the maker's product page if pocketability is a priority, since screen size alone does not fully predict it.

**Can I load library ebooks and library audiobooks on any of these?**

Library ebook borrowing availability depends on your library system and on each manufacturer's software ecosystem, not on the three specs compared in this guide. That is a software and account question rather than a hardware spec, and it is worth confirming with your own library's app before buying based on this comparison alone.

**Is the color screen worse for reading plain text than the black-and-white models?**

For black-and-white text, no — the color model in this comparison publishes the same 300 ppi for text as the other two, because the color filter layer only engages when color content is on screen. The resolution drop to 150 ppi applies specifically to color images and illustrations, not to standard text pages.
