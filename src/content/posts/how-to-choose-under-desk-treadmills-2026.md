Under-desk treadmills and walking pads all photograph the same way: a low black deck with a belt down the middle. What actually separates them is five published numbers — motor power, speed range, weight capacity, incline, and belt size — and manufacturers are inconsistent about which ones they publish at all. This guide walks through what each spec changes for someone walking at a desk, then lines up three models so you can match the figures to your own setup.

## Motor power decides continuous duty, not top speed

The horsepower figure on a walking pad's spec sheet is almost always a peak rating, not what the motor draws during normal use. A higher peak HP mostly means the motor works less hard to hold a given speed — that matters more for running than for slow walking, since running puts sustained load on the belt that walking does not.

At the walking speeds these machines are built for, the gap between a 2.0 HP and 3 HP motor is mostly headroom, not something you'll feel underfoot during a normal workday.

## Speed range is about walking, not running, for two of these three

Two of these three units top out under 4 mph — a brisk walk, not a jog. The third publishes a 6.2 mph ceiling and is explicitly built as a 2-in-1 unit for both walking and running. If your plan is only to walk while you work, the low end of the range matters more than the top: a minimum under 1 mph is what lets you hold a slow, sustainable pace during a call instead of stepping on and off.

## Weight capacity is a hard limit, not a suggestion

Published weight capacity covers the motor, belt, and frame together, and manufacturers test to that number, not past it — running a machine near or above its rated capacity is what shortens belt and motor life. The three units here range from 220 lb to 265 lb, a meaningful spread if you're near the lower figure. It has nothing to do with desk height or footprint — check it independently of the machine's size.

## Incline is the spec that changes the workout, not the desk experience

Only one of these three publishes an incline figure, and it's fixed at 5% rather than adjustable — no dialing it down for a call and back up for a dedicated walk. A fixed incline increases calorie burn at a given pace and adds load on the calves and Achilles compared with a flat belt, worth knowing if you have any lower-leg strain history.

The other two publish no incline at all, so the deck stays flat regardless of speed. That's a design choice, not an omission — a flat deck is quieter and simpler to fold, part of why the highest-capacity, fastest unit here skips incline entirely.

## Matching the specs to your desk setup

Rather than a single pick, the useful question is which constraint binds hardest for your space and your body.

- **You're near the top of a listed weight capacity** — check that figure first; it's a hard limit set by the manufacturer, not a rough guideline.
- **You want to walk and also run on the same machine** — speed range is the spec to optimise, since only a 2-in-1 unit's published ceiling clears a jogging pace.
- **Floor space is tight and the unit needs to store upright** — belt size and folded footprint matter more than motor power; a larger belt generally means a larger folded footprint too.
- **You want the workout to do more than a flat walk** — incline is the spec to look for, and currently only one tier here publishes it, and only as a fixed number.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I actually run on an under-desk treadmill, or only walk?**

It depends entirely on the published speed range and motor rating — most under-desk units are built and rated for walking speeds only, and pushing one past its rated top speed isn't something the manufacturer supports. Only a unit explicitly marketed and rated for both, like a 2-in-1 model with a running-range top speed, is built for it.

**How loud are these while on a work call?**

This is one place the spec sheet often can't help you: only one of the three manufacturers here publishes a decibel figure at all, and the other two simply don't list noise level as a spec. Where that's the case, owner reviews are a better source than the product page for gauging call-friendliness.

**Does a fixed incline actually burn more calories than a flat belt?**

Generally yes, for the same walking speed — added incline increases the physical work of walking, which is why manufacturers highlight it as a feature. But a fixed incline can't be turned off, so on a machine with only one incline setting, you're accepting that increased effort (and the added strain on your calves and Achilles) for every session, not just the ones where you want it.
