Cordless leaf blowers all show the same marketing photo of scattered leaves, but the spec sheets behind them vary more than the packaging suggests. Four published figures account for most of the real difference: air volume, air speed, battery and runtime, and noise level. This guide explains what each one physically changes, then lays three models side by side so you can weigh the numbers against your own yard.

## Air volume (CFM) measures how much air moves, not how far

CFM - cubic feet per minute - is the total volume of air the fan pushes, and it is the number most brands lead with. A higher CFM figure means more air moves through the nozzle each second, which clears wide areas of scattered, dry leaves quickly. It does not tell you how forcefully that air is aimed at a single wet, packed pile - that is a function of air speed, not volume.

The three units here span 650-780 CFM, a narrower range than the marketing suggests. At this range, the practical difference shows up in wide-area clearing speed on a driveway or open lawn, not in raw power at the nozzle tip.

## Air speed (MPH) is what actually dislodges wet or packed debris

Air speed measures how fast the air moves as it leaves the nozzle, and it is the figure that matters for anything stuck to a surface: wet leaves plastered to a walkway, clippings pressed into a gutter edge, or debris wedged against a fence line. A blower can move a large volume of air relatively slowly, or a smaller volume much faster - CFM and MPH are different design choices, not the same spec stated two ways.

One of the three manufacturers here does not publish an MPH figure at all, which is worth noting rather than guessing around: an unpublished spec is not evidence the number is bad, only that you cannot compare it on this axis before you buy.

## Battery voltage and runtime set how much yard you cover per charge

Voltage is a rough proxy for available power - these three run 40V, 56V and 60V systems, none directly comparable since amp-hours (Ah) also factor into total energy. What is directly comparable is the manufacturer's stated runtime on the included battery, which ranges 60-90 minutes here, plus the Ryobi's stated 2-hour combined runtime across its two included batteries.

Runtime published by the manufacturer assumes a specific fan speed setting, usually not the highest one, so treat the figure as a ceiling rather than a guarantee under heavy use.

## Noise level is the spec that decides where and when you can use it

Only one of the three models here publishes a decibel rating. A published dB figure lets you compare directly against local ordinances - many US municipalities cap residential lawn-equipment noise in the 60s-70s dB range at set distances - or against an HOA's quiet-hours policy. Without a published number, a relative marketing claim like "quieter than gas" describes a comparison to a different product category, not a figure you can hold against a noise ordinance.

If early-morning or shared-property use is part of your routine, a published dB figure is one of the few specs here that removes guesswork entirely.

## Matching the specs to your yard

- **You clear a large, open area of dry leaves** - air volume (CFM) is the spec to optimise, since it determines how much ground you cover per pass.
- **You deal with wet leaves or debris stuck to hard surfaces** - air speed (MPH) is what dislodges it, so check that figure is published before assuming higher CFM will do the job.
- **Your property is large enough that one charge needs to last** - runtime is the binding constraint, and the manufacturer's stated minutes is the only comparable figure across battery voltages.
- **You're near neighbors, on an HOA quiet-hours schedule, or blow early in the morning** - noise level is what to check first, and a published dB figure is more useful here than any relative marketing claim.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does higher CFM always mean a more powerful blower?**

Not on its own. CFM measures air volume, not force - a blower can move a large volume of air at a moderate speed, or a smaller volume at high speed. The two figures answer different questions: CFM for clearing wide areas quickly, MPH for dislodging debris stuck to a surface. Compare both where both are published.

**Why does the battery voltage vary so much between brands (40V, 56V, 60V)?**

Voltage is one input into total power, but manufacturers pair it with different amp-hour batteries, so a 40V system and a 60V system aren't directly comparable on voltage alone. The manufacturer's stated runtime and CFM/MPH figures are more useful points of comparison than voltage by itself.

**Is a cordless electric blower actually quieter than a gas one?**

Manufacturers commonly make this claim, and cordless electric motors are mechanically quieter than two-stroke gas engines by design. But "quieter than gas" is a relative marketing comparison, not a decibel figure - if your use case is governed by a specific noise ordinance or HOA rule stated in dB, you need a model that publishes an actual number, not a relative claim.
