Heated vests all promise the same thing on the box: a battery, a few heat settings, and warmth where you need it. The published specs that actually separate them are the battery platform behind the vest, how much capacity that battery holds, how many zones the wiring covers, and how long a charge lasts at the lowest setting. This guide walks through what each of those changes and what it doesn't, then lines up three vests so you can match the numbers to your own cold-weather routine.

## Battery platform decides what else you need to buy

A vest built around a proprietary battery, like ORORO's 7.38V pack, is self-contained: everything you need ships in the box, and replacing a worn-out cell means buying that brand's battery again. A vest built on a cordless tool platform, like Milwaukee's M12 or DEWALT's 20V MAX, uses the same batteries and chargers as that brand's drills and saws. If you already own that ecosystem, the vest effectively costs less because you're not paying for a redundant charger. If you don't, you're buying into a tool platform for one garment. Once the battery is charged, the platform makes no difference to how warm the vest feels — it only matters at purchase and at recharge time.

## Battery capacity is what runtime is built from — when it's published

Capacity, published in amp-hours (Ah), is the fuel tank the heating elements draw from. A larger Ah figure at the same voltage generally means longer runtime at a given setting, which is why ORORO's 4.8 Ah pack outlasts Milwaukee's 1.5 Ah CP1.5 battery on paper. The comparison only holds within similar voltage classes, and only works when the number is published — DEWALT's kit page lists the battery as included without stating its capacity, so there's nothing to calculate from for that one.

## Heat settings and zones change where you feel warm, not how warm you can get

The High/Medium/Low dial changes power draw, and therefore runtime, more than it changes peak temperature. Heating zones are the more physical spec: they describe where the heating wire is actually sewn into the fabric. A 2-zone layout concentrates the same battery output on the chest and back, while a 4-zone layout spreads it across the collar, upper back, and both front pockets — useful for hands and neck, but each zone draws a smaller share of the total wattage. None of the three vests here publishes a Fahrenheit figure per zone, so the setting name is the only lever you have to reason about intensity before buying.

## Runtime is only comparable at the same heat setting

Manufacturers publish runtime on the lowest heat setting, since it drains the battery slowest — expect roughly half that figure, sometimes less, on the highest setting. That's why ORORO's "up to 10 hours" and Milwaukee's "up to 6 hours" aren't purely about which battery is bigger; capacity and zone count both factor in. When a runtime figure isn't published at all, as with the DEWALT kit, there's no manufacturer number to plan an outdoor day around.

## Matching the specs to your cold-weather routine

- **You already own M12 or 20V MAX batteries** — battery platform is the spec to check first, since it can eliminate the cost of a redundant charger entirely.
- **You need warmth through a full outdoor shift** — battery capacity and runtime matter most; compare the published Ah and hour figures on the lowest setting before anything else.
- **You want heat exactly where you feel cold first** — heating zone count and placement is the spec to compare, since a 2-zone chest/back layout behaves differently from a 4-zone layout that includes pocket warmers.
- **You're buying for someone who won't track a battery gauge** — a simple 3-setting dial with no companion app is one less thing to manage than a platform built around app control.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do heated vests get hot enough to be a burn risk?**

Published heat-zone temperatures on vests like these generally top out in a range meant to feel warm against a base layer, not hot enough to burn skin in normal use. The bigger safety factor is wear and tear: manufacturers specify washing and charging instructions for a reason, since damaged heating wire is the actual risk, not the heat setting itself.

**Can I use a power-tool brand's vest without owning any of their tools?**

Yes — kits like the Milwaukee and DEWALT ones listed here include a battery and charger, so nothing else is required to start using them. The catch only shows up later: if you add more heated gear from the same brand, that battery investment carries forward the way it would with drills or saws, which is the appeal for people already in that ecosystem.

**Why do the same three heat settings feel different across brands?**

Low/Medium/High naming isn't standardized across manufacturers, so a vest's "High" is only guaranteed to be its hottest setting relative to its own "Low" — not a fixed temperature you can line up against a different brand's "High." That's something the spec table can't answer directly, which is why published runtime hours, tied to a specific battery and setting, end up being a more comparable number across brands than the setting names themselves.
