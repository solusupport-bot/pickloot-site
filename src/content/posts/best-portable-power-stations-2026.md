Portable power stations look like the same box in three sizes, and the marketing leads with one headline number. In practice five published specs account for almost everything that changes during an outage: how much energy is stored, how much the unit delivers at once, what it tolerates at startup, how many times it can be refilled, and how much it weighs. This guide explains what each one physically changes.

## Capacity tells you duration, not what will switch on

The watt-hour figure describes how much energy is in the battery. It says nothing about whether a given appliance will run — that is the output rating below. Two units with identical output ratings and different capacities power the same things, for different lengths of time.

Read it as division: capacity divided by the device's average draw in watts gives you hours, minus some margin for inverter losses. A refrigerator cycles rather than drawing continuously, so its average sits well below its nameplate figure, and sizing from the nameplate gives a rough floor rather than an answer. Capacity stops mattering on short outages: below a few hours, almost any unit here covers the essentials and you are buying output instead.

## Rated output is the spec that decides what works at all

Rated output, in watts, is the ceiling on continuous draw. Plug in something that pulls more and the unit shuts off — capacity is irrelevant to that outcome. The published figures here span an order of magnitude, 600 W to 6,000 W, and that range is the clearest dividing line in the category.

Surge output is the companion spec, and it is routinely overlooked. Anything with a motor or compressor — a fridge, a sump pump, a power tool — draws several times its running wattage for a fraction of a second at startup. A unit rated 600 W continuous with 1,500 W surge starts things a 600 W-flat rating would not. Check the surge column against the startup draw of the one appliance you actually care about.

## Cycle life is how many outages the unit survives

All three publish lithium iron phosphate chemistry, which is why the cycle ratings run into the thousands rather than the hundreds older lithium-ion packs carried. A cycle is one full discharge and recharge. EcoFlow publishes 4,000 cycles to 80%-or-better capacity; the other two publish 3,000-plus.

For most households this spec never binds — if the unit comes out four times a year, 3,000 cycles is several lifetimes. It starts to matter if you cycle it daily, for off-grid use or to shift solar production into the evening. One related gap is worth naming: recharge speed is published by two of the three here (70 and 56 minutes to full at their maximum inputs) and not published at all by the third.

## Weight is what makes "portable" either true or nominal

The category name covers a 9.5 lb unit and a 132 lb one. Both are called portable; only one is carried. This spec quietly determines where the unit lives, and therefore whether it is with you when the power goes out.

Under roughly 15 lb a unit travels — a car, a tent, a second floor. Around 25 to 35 lb it moves between rooms with effort and stays home. Past 100 lb it needs a permanent spot, and the question shifts from where to carry it to how far the extension cords reach.

## Matching the specs to your outage

The useful question is which constraint binds hardest where you live.

- **Outages last a few hours, a few times a year** — output and surge matter; capacity past a few hundred watt-hours buys duration you will not use.
- **A refrigerator has to keep running** — surge output first, since a compressor that will not start makes capacity moot, then capacity for duration.
- **The unit has to travel** — weight is the binding spec, and everything else is downstream of what you will carry.
- **You need 240 V appliances** — only the highest output tier publishes 240 V outlets at all, which narrows the field before any other spec does.
- **You cycle it most days** — rated cycle life, the spec that converts directly into years of service.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a portable power station run a full-size refrigerator?**

Two separate specs decide it, not one. Surge output has to exceed the compressor's startup draw or the fridge never comes on, and capacity determines how many hours it runs after that. Check both against the nameplate figures on the back of your own refrigerator.

**Do I need a solar panel to make one useful during a long outage?**

Not for a short outage — charging from AC beforehand covers it. Past the unit's capacity, solar input is the only way to extend it, but panels are not universal across brands. Check the station's published solar input range and connector type against the specific panel.

**Is a portable power station a substitute for a whole-home generator?**

They solve overlapping but different problems. A battery unit runs indoors, silently, with no fuel or exhaust, limited by its stored watt-hours. A fuel generator has effectively unlimited duration as long as you can refill it, but cannot run indoors. The question is whether your outages are measured in hours or in days.
