Digital photo frames all list a screen size and a WiFi badge, and on a shelf they look close to interchangeable. What actually separates them are two specs marketing copy tends to bury: how photos get stored, and whether there's a ceiling on that, plus how you interact with the frame once photos are on it. This guide reads through both of those, along with what resolution and video support do and don't change.

## Screen resolution is nearly identical across this category

All three frames here land at 1280 x 800, the HD resolution most 10-inch photo frames ship with regardless of price band. That's well below what a modern phone camera captures, but the gap rarely shows at typical viewing distance — a frame sits across a room, not six inches from your face. Where resolution matters is upscaling: a photo shot at much higher resolution gets downsampled to fit the panel, so detail is lost to the frame's spec, not the source photo. A manufacturer that doesn't publish a resolution figure at all is a bigger flag than a modest one — it usually points to older panel stock.

## Storage model decides whether you'll ever hit a wall

This is where the three genuinely diverge, and it's a model difference, not just a number. Skylight ships with fixed internal storage: what's published is what you get, and near the roughly 8,000-photo mark you're deleting to make room. Aura goes the opposite direction — cloud storage with no published capacity limit, so there's no ceiling to plan around, but photos live on Aura's servers indefinitely rather than on a physical chip in your living room. Nixplay splits the difference: its free tier caps out at 500MB of photos and 500MB of video, a genuinely small allotment, and unlimited storage only arrives with a paid subscription. Read "cloud storage" carefully on any listing — it isn't automatically unlimited just because it isn't a fixed chip.

## Touchscreen vs. touch bar changes how you use the frame, not what you see on it

Two of these three have a real touchscreen: tap the glass to pinch, crop, or advance photos directly. Aura instead uses a touch bar along the frame's edge — a strip you swipe or tap for basic navigation, while actual editing and captions happen in the companion app on your phone. Neither approach is more capable; they're just different places where the interaction happens. If you want to hand the frame to a relative who won't install an app, a true touchscreen removes that step. If you'd rather manage everything from your own phone, the touch-bar approach is arguably less to explain to someone else.

## Video and motion support is the spec sheets tend to bury

Not every frame plays video, and the manufacturers that don't support it generally say so plainly rather than staying silent — Skylight's documentation lists supported formats as photo-only (JPEG, BMP, PNG, GIF) and explicitly notes the device has no camera or microphone. Aura supports live photos and video clips up to 30 seconds. Nixplay supports 15-second clips on its free plan, extending to 2 minutes with a paid Nixplay Plus subscription. If you're populating a frame mostly with Live Photos or clips from a family group chat, this spec — more than resolution — decides whether those actually play back.

## Matching the specs to your household

- **You're buying it for someone who won't install an app** — touch interface is the spec to check first; a true touchscreen lets them manage photos without a phone in the loop.
- **You upload constantly and don't want to manage space** — storage model matters more than any GB figure; a published unlimited cloud tier removes the ceiling entirely.
- **You mostly send short video clips or Live Photos** — video and motion support is the spec that decides whether those play at all, not resolution.
- **You want to avoid a subscription decision later** — check whether "unlimited" storage is actually free or gated behind a paid plan, since two of the three tie full capacity to a subscription tier.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do digital photo frames need WiFi to work?**

All three frames here require a WiFi connection to receive new photos — that's how images get uploaded remotely by family members. Photos already stored on the frame typically keep displaying if WiFi drops, but nothing new arrives until it reconnects, so a spotty router is a bigger practical issue day to day than any single spec on this table.

**Does a bigger screen mean a better picture?**

Not necessarily — screen size and resolution are separate specs, and in this category they don't move together the way they might on a TV. A larger frame at the same 1280 x 800 resolution spreads the same number of pixels over more surface area, which can look slightly softer up close even though the screen itself is the larger published figure.

**Can more than one person upload photos to the same frame?**

This isn't something a spec sheet answers directly, but it's worth checking before buying. Aura and Nixplay both support multiple contributors adding photos remotely through their apps, which is often the actual feature gift-givers care about most. Skylight supports this too, but with fixed local storage, more contributors simply means reaching that ceiling sooner.
