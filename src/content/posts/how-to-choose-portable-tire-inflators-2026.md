Tire inflators look interchangeable in a listing photo — a motor, a hose, a gauge — but the manufacturer spec sheet is where they actually diverge. Four published figures account for most of the practical difference: max pressure, power source, airflow (how fast it actually moves air), and weight. Here's what each one changes, with three inflators' own published numbers laid out side by side.

## Max pressure is a ceiling, not a speed

The PSI figure on the box tells you the highest pressure the pump can build, not how quickly it gets there. A passenger car tire typically runs 30-35 PSI, so a 120 PSI-rated tool and a 150 PSI-rated one both clear that with room to spare. The ceiling starts to matter for high-pressure use cases: some truck, trailer, and off-road tires are spec'd well above passenger-car pressures, and a compressor rated closer to its own maximum has less margin left for accuracy or motor strain.

Treat max pressure as a pass/fail check against what you're actually inflating, then look at airflow to judge speed.

## Power source trades cord-length freedom for a runtime you often can't check

Corded 12V inflators plug into a vehicle's accessory outlet and draw power as long as the engine is running or the battery has charge to spare — the VIAIR here pulls up to 30 amps, which is worth knowing if the outlet shares a fuse with anything else. Cordless inflators run off a rechargeable battery pack, which means no cord to route through a window or door, but also a runtime that depends on a battery most manufacturers don't publish a duty-cycle figure for. Milwaukee's own spec sheet for the M12 inflator lists pressure and output but no run-time-per-charge number — a real gap in the published data, not an oversight in this guide.

## Airflow predicts inflation time - when manufacturers actually publish it

This is the spec that answers "how long will this take," and it's also the one brands report the least consistently. Some publish CFM (cubic feet per minute) at a specific pressure, some publish a duty-cycle percentage instead, and some publish neither and describe speed only in marketing language like "fast inflation." In this set, Milwaukee publishes CFM at two pressures and a real-world fill time for a car tire; VIAIR publishes CFM at 0 PSI plus a duty-cycle rating; AstroAI's product page doesn't publish an airflow or duty-cycle figure at all. That gap is exactly what "Not published" is for in this table — it's an honest account of what the manufacturer disclosed, not a missing review.

## Weight determines where the inflator actually lives

A sub-3-pound corded inflator fits in a glovebox or door pocket and stays there unnoticed. A near-11-pound unit with a bigger motor and a higher duty cycle usually lives in a trunk or a truck bed, because it's built to run longer without overheating, and that hardware adds mass. Weight isn't published on every page either — Milwaukee's spec sheet for the M12 inflator lists pressure and battery type but no weight figure - so it's worth checking a retailer listing if this is the deciding factor for you.

## Matching the specs to your trunk

- **Glovebox space is tightest** — weight and a compact corded form factor are the specs to optimize; the lightest published figure here is under 2.5 lbs.
- **You don't want to route a cord to the outlet** — power source is the constraint; a cordless battery system trades that convenience for an unpublished runtime.
- **You're inflating truck, trailer, or RV tires** — max pressure headroom and duty cycle matter more than portability here.
- **You inflate several tires back-to-back** — airflow and duty cycle, since a lower-rated motor may need cool-down breaks a higher-duty-cycle unit doesn't.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher max PSI rating mean faster inflation?**

No. Max PSI is a ceiling the pump can reach, not a rate. Two inflators can share the same 150 PSI rating and still fill a tire at very different speeds — that's determined by airflow (CFM) at the pressure you're actually filling to, not by the maximum the unit is capable of.

**Why don't all three inflators report airflow the same way?**

They genuinely don't, and the spec table alone can't resolve it. Milwaukee reports CFM at two pressures plus a real-world fill time, VIAIR reports CFM at 0 PSI alongside a duty-cycle percentage, and AstroAI's page doesn't publish an airflow figure at all. There's no published conversion between "CFM at 0 PSI" and "minutes to fill a car tire," so a cross-brand airflow comparison has to stop at what each manufacturer chose to disclose.

**Is a cordless inflator underpowered compared to a corded one?**

Not necessarily, but in this set the cordless option is rated to a lower max pressure (120 PSI versus 150 PSI) than either corded option. Whether that's a general pattern or specific to these three models isn't something the spec sheets settle - it would take checking additional cordless models to know.
