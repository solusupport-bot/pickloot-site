Outdoor security cameras look interchangeable in photos — a weatherproof housing with a lens — but four published specs account for most of what differs once one is mounted outside: video resolution, field of view, night vision range, and power source. This guide explains what each one changes, then lays three current models side by side so you can match the numbers to your own porch, driveway, or yard.

## Video resolution changes how much you can crop in later, not how sharp live view looks

At native size on a phone screen, 1080p and 2K footage look similar. The difference shows up when you crop into a saved clip to check a face at the end of the driveway or a plate at the curb. A 2K sensor (2048 x 1080) captures roughly twice the pixel data of 1080p, tolerating a much tighter crop before the image breaks into blocky artifacts.

If the camera sits right at a doorway and you only need to confirm who rang the bell, 1080p is enough. If it's watching a driveway or street, where identification happens well after the footage is cropped, the extra resolution is what actually pays off.

## Field of view decides how many cameras you need, not how far they can see

Manufacturers don't measure this the same way. Some publish a single diagonal angle; others split it into separate horizontal and vertical figures, so a direct side-by-side comparison is approximate rather than exact. The number controls coverage width — wider horizontal angle means fewer cameras needed across a yard — and separately, how much of a visitor's body appears in frame up close, which comes from the vertical angle specifically.

A camera reporting 140° horizontal and a narrower 80° vertical will frame someone standing at the door differently than one reporting a single wide diagonal number. Neither format is wrong; they're just not directly comparable without converting one to the other, which the manufacturers don't do for you.

## Night vision range is the spec most often left unpublished — and the gap itself is informative

Night vision on these cameras comes from infrared or near-infrared LEDs that illuminate the scene so the sensor can register motion in the dark. The range, in feet, tells you how far from the mount a person or vehicle shows up as an identifiable shape rather than a vague blob. When a vendor doesn't publish this number, it usually means real-world performance varies too much with ambient light, mounting height, and firmware to hold up as one figure. A published number like 25 feet is useful specifically for placement — it tells you whether the camera actually covers the gate or driveway you're pointing it at after dark, not just during the day.

## Power source determines the ongoing chore, not just the install

Battery-only cameras need no wiring, but require periodic battery swaps or recharging. Some vendors publish an estimated runtime for this — Blink rates its included AA lithium cells at up to two years under default settings, possible to quote precisely because the battery chemistry is fixed and well characterized. Others, on built-in rechargeable packs, publish only a relative claim against a prior model rather than an absolute number, likely because usage patterns and temperature swing the real figure too much for one headline claim. Wired or AC-powered setups skip recharging entirely but need an outlet or in-wall wiring near the mount — a bigger decision at install time and a non-issue afterward.

## Matching the specs to your entry point

- **You need to identify faces or plates at a distance** — resolution is the spec to optimize, not field of view.
- **Your mounting point is far from an outlet** — power source matters most, and whether the vendor actually publishes a runtime figure or only a relative claim.
- **You're covering a wide yard with a single camera** — field of view, keeping in mind diagonal and horizontal/vertical figures aren't directly comparable.
- **The spot stays dark well into the evening** — night vision range, and treat an unpublished figure as a real unknown rather than assuming it matches a camera that does publish one.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a wider field of view mean a camera can see farther into the dark?**

No — field of view and night vision range are unrelated specs. A wide-angle lens captures more width at a given distance; it doesn't project infrared light or resolve detail any farther than a narrower lens would. A camera can have a very wide field of view and a short night vision range, or the reverse.

**Why do some cameras publish an exact battery life while others don't?**

Cameras using replaceable, non-rechargeable batteries — like two AA lithium cells — can quote a controlled lab-test figure because that chemistry is fixed and well understood. Cameras with built-in rechargeable packs vary more with real-world usage and temperature, which may be why some vendors give only a relative comparison to an earlier model instead of a number you can plan around.

**Do any of these work without a paid subscription?**

All three route recorded footage through a cloud service tied to the brand's own app, and extended clip history or continuous recording generally needs a paid plan after an initial trial. Live viewing and basic motion alerts typically work without one, but subscription terms change between models and regions, so check the manufacturer's own site before buying rather than assuming it matches another model in this guide.
