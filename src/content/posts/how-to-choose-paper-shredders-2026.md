Paper shredders all show the same stock photo — curling ribbons of paper falling into a bin — and the box copy leans on words like "heavy-duty" instead of numbers. Four published specs actually explain why one unit costs more than another: how many sheets it takes per pass, what security level it's rated to, how long the motor runs before it needs to cool down, and how big the bin is. Here's what each one changes.

## Sheet capacity is tested under ideal conditions, not yours

The sheets-per-pass figure comes from a lab feeding flat, dry, standard 20 lb. paper with no staples or folds. Real paper rarely arrives that way — a folded bank statement or a stapled packet behaves like more sheets than it is, and most manufacturers quietly expect you to shred under the rated number for that reason. Treat the published figure as a ceiling you approach, not a target you load every time, and compare it against the thickest stack you actually shred, not an average one.

## Security level describes particle size, not toughness

The P-rating (P-1 through P-7, from the DIN 66399 standard) measures how small the shredded particles are, which is a proxy for how hard reconstruction would be — not how sturdy the machine is. A strip-cut shredder at the low end of the scale produces long, readable ribbons; a cross-cut unit around P-4 produces confetti-sized pieces that are impractical to reassemble by hand; a micro-cut unit at P-5 or higher turns a page into particles closer to dust. Most home paper — junk mail, old receipts — doesn't need the top of the scale. Anything carrying a Social Security number, an account number, or a legal signature is the category this spec is actually protecting.

## Continuous run time is a thermal limit, not a speed setting

A shredder's motor heats up as it runs, and the "minutes on, minutes off" figure is how long it can go before it has to stop and cool before the next use. A budget motor rated for two minutes on and a 30-minute cooldown is built for occasional, light use; a rating of "continuous duty" means the motor is built to run indefinitely without that enforced break. This spec has nothing to do with how fast or cleanly the blades cut — it only governs how much total shredding you can do in one sitting before the machine stops you.

## Waste bin capacity decides how often you empty it, not how well it shreds

The bin size is a housekeeping spec, not a performance one. A small bin on a high-capacity, continuous-duty motor will fill long before the motor needs a break, which defeats some of the point of that motor. A large bin on a low-capacity unit mostly just means less frequent trips to empty it. Check this spec against how the other three combine for your use, not on its own.

## Matching the specs to your workload

- **You shred rarely, a few pages at a time** — run time barely matters here; just check sheet capacity against your actual stack size.
- **The shredder will run for stretches throughout a workday** — continuous run time is the spec to optimize; a cooldown-limited motor will interrupt a shared office repeatedly.
- **You're destroying anything with an account number, SSN, or signature on it** — security level is what to check, and a published P-4 or higher is the number to look for.
- **You don't want to stop and empty the bin mid-session** — bin capacity is the spec to size up, independent of the motor's capacity.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher security (P) rating always mean a better shredder?**

Not on its own. Finer cutting geometry that produces smaller particles generally also means fewer sheets per pass and a slower feed, so a high P-rating often trades off against the capacity and run-time specs above. Match the rating to what you're actually destroying rather than defaulting to the highest number available.

**Why do some shredders not publish a security/P-level at all?**

Getting a model independently rated against the DIN 66399 standard costs money, and manufacturers of basic, low-capacity shredders often skip it because the target buyer isn't shredding regulated or highly sensitive documents. An unpublished P-level isn't evidence the shredder is insecure — it means that axis simply wasn't tested, which is itself useful to know before you rely on it for sensitive paperwork.

**Can any cross-cut shredder handle CDs, DVDs, or credit cards?**

Only if the manufacturer explicitly lists that capability — the cutting head and motor torque needed to shred rigid plastic or a disc are different from what's needed for paper, and running unsupported media through a paper-only unit can dull the blades or jam the mechanism. Check the product's own stated media list rather than assuming cross-cut paper capability extends to it.
