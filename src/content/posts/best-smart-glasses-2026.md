Smart glasses have been one of the more closely watched product stories of 2026, and the pace picked up when Meta paused the international rollout of its display model earlier this year, citing demand that outpaced supply. That kind of attention has pulled a wider range of buyers into the category, from people who just want hands-free audio to those chasing a built-in display. We researched three real options spanning that range, based on published specs and owner and reviewer feedback rather than a lab test.

## Amazon Echo Frames (3rd Gen)

The Echo Frames are the simplest and least expensive way into the category: open-ear speakers, a microphone array for Alexa, and no camera or display at all. That last part is a feature for some buyers, since there's nothing recording video and nothing to distract you visually while walking or driving. Reviewers note the battery runs up to about six hours per charge, and the open-ear design means you can still hear traffic and conversations around you. The tradeoff is audio bleed — at higher volumes, people standing nearby can pick up what you're listening to, and the frames only handle audio tasks, not photos or AI vision.

## Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2)

The Gen 2 Ray-Ban Meta glasses sit in the middle: a 12MP camera capable of 3K video, Meta's Live AI that can look at what you're looking at and answer questions about it, and up to 8 hours of mixed-use battery with another 48 hours available from the charging case. At 32GB of onboard storage, they can hold roughly 500 photos or 100 short videos without needing a paired phone nearby to offload footage. They carry an IPX4 rating, so they handle a light rain shower but aren't built for swimming. There's still no display here — every AI response comes through the speakers, not your field of view — which is the main thing separating this tier from the one above it.

## Meta Ray-Ban Display

The Display model is the newest and most expensive tier, built around a 600x600 pixel color display tucked into the right lens with a 90Hz refresh rate and brightness that automatically adjusts for sunlight. Instead of a phone-style touchpad, it pairs with a Meta Neural Band worn on the forearm, which reads electrical signals from your muscles to register pinches and swipes without anyone seeing you gesture. Battery life runs up to 6 hours mixed use, stretching to about 30 hours with the case, and the frames accept prescription lenses from -4.00 to +4.00. The catch, beyond the price, is availability — Meta has paused parts of its international rollout to manage demand, so stock and shipping windows can vary by region.

## Bottom line

If you want hands-free audio and Alexa without a camera in the mix, the Echo Frames are the cheapest way in. Most buyers who want photos, video, and an AI assistant that can actually see what they see will land on the Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2. The Display model only makes sense if you specifically want an in-lens screen for maps, messages, and captions and are fine paying more than double the Gen 2's price for it.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do any of these require a monthly subscription?**
No — all three work with a free companion app for setup and basic features. Meta and Amazon have both offered optional paid AI tiers with extra capabilities in the past, so it's worth checking the current app listing before buying if that matters to you.

**Can I wear these if I need prescription lenses?**
The Meta Ray-Ban Display explicitly supports prescription inserts from -4.00 to +4.00 diopters. Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2) and Echo Frames also offer prescription-ready frame options, but you'll typically need to order lenses separately through an optical partner rather than getting them in the box.

**Which one has the best camera?**
Between these three, only the Ray-Ban Meta Gen 2 and the Display model have cameras — the Echo Frames are audio-only. The Gen 2's 12MP sensor and 3K video capability are the same core camera used across Meta's Ray-Ban lineup, so photo and video quality should be similar between the two Meta models.
