Video doorbells all show the same marketing photo — a phone screen with a visitor on the porch — which makes them look interchangeable until you compare what each one publishes. Four specs account for most of the real difference: video resolution, field of view, how the unit is powered, and whether it can save footage without a monthly subscription. This guide explains what each one changes, then lays three models side by side so you can match the numbers to your own front door.

## Video resolution changes what you can identify, not just how sharp the picture looks

Pixel count matters less than shape here. A doorbell mounted at chest height photographs a tall, narrow scene — a person's full body, a package on the mat — and a frame built for that shape can beat a higher pixel count in a wide-screen ratio. Nest's 960×1280 frame is a portrait 3:4 ratio aimed at that head-to-toe view; Ring's 1536p and Blink's 1440p are both higher pixel counts, but neither publishes an aspect ratio built around a standing visitor.

In practice, resolution differences between these three mostly show up when zooming into a recorded clip afterward — reading a face or a delivery label. For a quick glance at who's at the door, all three are sharp enough.

## Field of view decides whether you see the porch or just a face

Two of the three models here publish the same 150°-by-150° figure, wide enough to catch a package left to the side of the frame, not just someone standing directly in front of the lens. Nest's 145° diagonal is measured differently — one diagonal figure rather than separate horizontal and vertical numbers — which makes a direct comparison harder even though the numbers look close.

A narrower field of view mostly costs you the edges: a car in the driveway, a package set down out of the doorbell's direct sightline. If your entryway is recessed or your porch is deep, check the degree figure against your own doorway width before anything else.

## Power source determines how the doorbell dies, and how you bring it back

All three are cordless-capable, but the batteries behave differently. Blink uses two swappable AA cells rated for up to two years — when they die, you replace them on the spot with cells from a drawer. Ring's Quick Release Battery Pack detaches for charging, so the doorbell body stays on the door while the pack recharges indoors, at the cost of live coverage in the meantime. Nest's 6 Ah lithium-ion cell is built into the unit itself, so recharging means unmounting the whole doorbell rather than swapping a part.

None of this affects video quality — it affects how much attention the doorbell needs over a year of ownership, and whether a dead battery leaves your door uncovered for hours or days.

## Video storage is the spec that decides whether the doorbell works without a monthly bill

This is where the three diverge most. Blink can save clips locally through a Sync Module 2 and a USB flash drive, no subscription required — though the module is a separate purchase. Ring's published materials describe subscription-gated detection features and cloud history through Ring Protect plans, with no local-storage option listed. Nest defaults to a free three-hour rolling event history, extendable with a Nest Aware subscription, but also without a published continuous local-storage path.

If keeping clips without an ongoing plan matters, this is the one spec worth confirming on the manufacturer's current product page before buying — subscription terms change more often than hardware specs.

## Matching the specs to your entryway

- **You don't want a recurring bill** — storage is the spec to check first; only one of these three publishes a no-subscription local-storage path.
- **Your porch is deep or your entry is recessed** — field of view is the binding constraint, since a narrow angle crops out the edges of a wide doorstep.
- **You don't want to think about charging** — power source matters most; swappable batteries mean no downtime, a built-in battery means the whole unit comes off the door to charge.
- **You already run other smart-home devices** — check which ecosystem (Ring's app, Google Home) the doorbell is built for, since none of these three cross-integrate.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do video doorbells work if the WiFi goes down?**

No — all three connect over WiFi to send alerts and stream live video, so a router outage or a weak signal at the door means no notifications and no live view until the connection returns. This is a limitation of the category, not something any published spec sheet discloses as a number.

**Does resolution or field of view matter more for catching a package left at the door?**

Field of view, in most cases. A package left just outside a narrow frame won't be recorded at any resolution, while a wide-angle camera at lower resolution still captures the event, just with less fine detail on zoom.

**Can I install a battery-powered video doorbell myself?**

Battery-powered models like these three are generally designed for self-installation with a screwdriver and no wiring, since they don't connect to existing doorbell chime wires. Replacing an existing wired doorbell is a different installation path that may involve your home's low-voltage transformer — worth checking against the specific model's own installation guide first.
