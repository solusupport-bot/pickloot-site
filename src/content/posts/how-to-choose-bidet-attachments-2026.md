Every bidet attachment on the shelf promises "a fresher clean," and out of the box they look nearly identical — a seat-mounted unit with a nozzle and a control knob or dial. What actually separates them is in the spec sheet, not the marketing copy: whether the water is heated, how the pressure is adjusted, whether the nozzle cleans itself, and whether the seat itself is heated. Those four published specs decide which unit fits your bathroom.

## Water temperature changes comfort, not the cleaning itself

A non-electric attachment that connects only to the cold line sprays water at whatever temperature your tap runs — cooler in winter, closer to room temperature the rest of the year. A dual-temperature attachment like the mid-tier model here solves that by also connecting to the sink's warm water line, so the spray temperature tracks your water heater rather than the season. An electric seat goes a step further and heats the water internally, so temperature stays consistent regardless of the water heater or the weather outside.

None of this changes how well the unit cleans. It changes whether using it in January feels the same as using it in July, which matters more in a cold climate or a bathroom on an exterior wall than it does anywhere else.

## Pressure control is about adjustability, not a published number

None of the three manufacturers here publish a PSI figure — pressure is described as "adjustable" rather than quantified, and that's typical for the category. What is published is the mechanism: a turn knob, a side-arm dial, or a remote. Physically, low pressure under-cleans and very high pressure can be uncomfortable, so the spec worth checking is how finely you can dial it in, not a number you won't find on any of these product pages.

Worth noting for the premium seat specifically: manufacturers that publish detailed nozzle-position and dryer-level counts often stay silent on pressure and water-temperature figures. An unpublished number here isn't a red flag — it's just not something these brands disclose.

## Nozzle self-cleaning is hygiene, not a cosmetic feature

All three units here publish a self-cleaning nozzle, which mechanically means the nozzle rinses its own surface immediately before it extends and again after it retracts, so it isn't sitting in the bowl or exposed with residue on it between uses. That's now close to standard across this category, so it's less of a differentiator than it used to be — the real difference is nozzle count and position. The budget and mid-range units each use a single nozzle; the premium seat uses two stainless steel nozzles across seven adjustable positions, which is a wash-coverage spec, not a hygiene one.

## Seat heating only exists on an electric seat, and that's the real dividing line

Seat heating isn't available on either non-electric attachment in this comparison — it requires a powered unit, and the premium seat here runs on 120V/60Hz household power with a 3.5-foot cord. That same power connection is what also enables the 4-level warm air dryer bundled with it. So "heated seat" is really shorthand for a bigger decision: whether you're willing to add a grounded, ideally GFCI-protected outlet near the toilet, which the two attachment-style units never require.

## Matching the specs to your bathroom

- **No outlet near the toilet** — a non-electric attachment is the constraint that decides everything else, since seat heating and warm air drying aren't available without power.
- **Cold climate or an unheated bathroom** — water temperature is the spec to check first; dual-temperature or electric heating stops the spray from tracking the season.
- **Multiple household members with different preferences** — look for a programmable remote or per-user memory, which the electric seat here supports for two users.
- **Want to cut down on toilet paper use** — the warm air dryer is the spec that does that job, and on the market today it comes bundled only with electric seats.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a plumber to install a bidet attachment?**

Most attachments, including both non-electric units compared here, install under the existing toilet seat using an included braided hose and T-valve, with no plumbing modification — TUSHY states an install time under 8.5 minutes. An electric seat adds one more requirement: a grounded outlet, ideally on a GFCI circuit, within reach of the cord.

**Why would anyone choose the cold-water-only option if warm is more comfortable?**

Price and simplicity. A cold-water-only attachment has no connection to a warm line or a power source, so there's less that can leak, need an outlet, or eventually fail electronically. It's a reasonable trade-off if the bathroom stays warm enough that tap-temperature water isn't uncomfortable, or if the extra hardware isn't worth it for occasional use.

**Will a bidet attachment fit my toilet?**

The spec table doesn't cover this, and it's worth checking before buying: attachments are built to fit under a standard round or elongated seat with the seat bolts in a typical position, and manufacturers publish compatibility as a percentage rather than a guarantee — TUSHY states it fits 95% of American toilets. Wall-mounted, unusually narrow, or non-standard toilets are the cases most likely to need a return.
