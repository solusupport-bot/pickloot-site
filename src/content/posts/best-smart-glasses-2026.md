Smart glasses look like one category and behave like three. From the outside, a pair of audio frames, a pair of camera glasses and a pair with a screen in the lens are all just glasses with electronics in the arms. Five published specs account for nearly all of the difference: how long a charge lasts, what the case adds, whether there is a camera, whether there is a display, and what water the frame is rated for.

## Battery is two numbers, and the second one is the useful one

Manufacturers publish a per-charge figure and a case figure, and they are not measured the same way. Meta says Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2) lasts up to eight hours in typical use but only five with continuous audio streaming, and publishes Meta Ray-Ban Display at up to six hours of mixed use. Amazon publishes no mixed-use figure for Echo Frames at all — its six hours is continuous media playback or talk time, a different and harder test.

Because "typical" and "mixed" are the maker's own definitions, the per-charge number is only loosely comparable across brands. The case figure is firmer: Ray-Ban Meta's case is published at 48 additional hours, or eight full frame charges, and the Display's adds 24 hours for 30 hours total. Amazon documents a charging stand rather than a battery case for Echo Frames, which makes a day away from a socket a different proposition.

## The camera is the line between two kinds of product

Echo Frames have no camera. That is a specification, not an omission — it is what makes them wearable where camera glasses draw objections.

Where a camera exists, the numbers separate less than you would guess. Both Meta products publish a 12 MP sensor, but the video specs diverge in an unintuitive direction: Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2) at 3K Ultra HD, Meta Ray-Ban Display at 1440 x 1920 at 30 fps. The higher tier is not automatically the higher figure. Both publish 32 GB of storage; Amazon publishes no storage figure for Echo Frames, consistent with a device that stores nothing.

## The display is the one spec that changes what the device is for

Only one of the three has a screen. Meta publishes it as 600 x 600 pixels, full colour, 20° field of view, monocular — in one lens rather than both. Refresh rate and brightness are not published on any manufacturer page, which matters because outdoor legibility is what a brightness figure would tell you.

Monocular and 20° are the two words to sit with. A 20° field of view is a small window in part of your vision, suited to a turn arrow, a caption line or a message preview — not to video or a phone screen. In one lens rather than two, it is glanceable rather than immersive. The question is whether the information you want is the kind you glance at, since everything else these glasses do, the other tiers do through audio.

## The IP rating is where all three land in the same place

All three publish IPX4. That covers splashing water from any direction — rain on a walk, sweat on a run — but not submersion, and no manufacturer here claims a dust rating at all: the X in IPX4 means dust was not tested.

So water resistance does not separate these three, and it stops mattering above a low bar. One exception worth knowing: Meta publishes IPX7 for the Neural Band worn on the forearm with the Display, a higher rating than the glasses it controls.

## Matching the specs to your day

- **You want to hear, not see** — battery per charge and case capacity, since audio use is what drains these fastest.
- **You are away from a socket all day** — the case figure, not the frame figure; the spread between the three is large here.
- **You want to capture what you are looking at** — published camera resolution, read carefully, because it does not track price tier.
- **Colleagues or family object to cameras** — the camera row is the whole decision, and one option publishes "none".
- **You want directions or captions in view** — the display row, and accept that refresh rate and brightness are unpublished.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do any of these require a monthly subscription?**

All three work with a free companion app for setup and core features. Meta and Amazon have both offered optional paid AI tiers alongside their assistants, so check the current app listing before buying if that matters. Nothing in the specs above sits behind a subscription.

**Can I wear these if I need prescription lenses?**

All three lines offer prescription-ready frame options, but lenses are normally ordered separately through an optical partner rather than supplied in the box. Each line publishes a supported prescription range that is narrower than what a standard optician can make, so check the current range on the manufacturer's page against your own prescription before ordering.

**Why does the model with a display have lower video specs?**

Because the display and the camera compete for the same power and thermal budget in a frame that still has to sit on a nose. Meta publishes 3K video for Ray-Ban Meta (Gen 2) and 1440 x 1920 at 30 fps for Meta Ray-Ban Display. Tier ordering here tracks capability added, not every spec raised.
