Dash cams are sold on resolution, and resolution is the spec that matters least often. What separates one unit from another is duller: how many directions it records at once, how wide each lens sees, and whether the file carries a location and speed stamp when someone else's insurer asks for it. Four published specs cover almost all of that.

## Resolution decides plate legibility, and only at distance

Every camera here records a clear picture of the car in front of you. The resolution figure governs something narrower: how far back you can still resolve a license plate. Going from 2304x1296P to 2592x1944P is roughly a 50% increase in vertical pixels, the axis plate characters sit on, and 4K roughly doubles the entry figure again.

Where it stops mattering is close range and daylight. At two car lengths in good light, 1296P already resolves a plate. The gap opens at four or five car lengths, at night, and when a vehicle crosses the frame rather than sitting in it.

## Channel count is the spec most people get wrong

Channels means lenses recording simultaneously, and it is the one axis where these three are genuinely different products rather than grades of the same one. A single-channel camera sees forward, which covers the most common collision type and nothing else.

Two things fall outside it. Rear coverage matters if you are rear-ended or someone reverses into you in a lot — that happens entirely outside a forward lens's view. Cabin coverage matters almost exclusively for rideshare and delivery work, which is why the tri-channel format is common there and rare elsewhere. The trade is install effort and storage: three lenses means routing cable to the rear glass, and three streams fill a card several times faster than one.

## Viewing angle is not a bigger-is-better number

The front angles here run 130°, 160° and 158°, and the ordering is worth pausing on: the widest published front lens belongs to the mid-tier camera, not the premium one. A wider lens captures more of the adjacent lanes and the sidewalk, which is where a cyclist or a merging car appears before it becomes your problem.

The cost is that a fixed sensor spread over a wider scene gives each object fewer pixels, and wide lenses stretch detail toward the frame edges. Angle and resolution pull against each other: 130° concentrates the sensor on what is directly ahead, 160° trades some of that density for peripheral coverage. On a multi-lane commute the peripheral view tends to earn its keep; on a narrow two-lane road it mostly records hedges.

## GPS is what turns footage into evidence

This is the cleanest yes-or-no spec in the category. A camera with GPS writes speed and coordinates into the clip alongside the video. A camera without it produces video that shows what happened but cannot establish where or how fast.

That matters specifically in disputes, because the common counter-claim in a collision is about speed, and video alone is poor evidence of it. Two of the three cameras publish built-in GPS; on the Vantrue E1 the receiver lives in the mount, so a replacement mount has to be the GPS version. If nobody is likely to contest your account of events, GPS is a feature you will never look at.

## Matching the specs to your driving

- **You park on the street overnight** — resolution and viewing angle, since the footage you need is of a vehicle passing through the frame rather than hitting you head-on. All three also need a separately sold hardwire kit before parking surveillance runs.
- **You drive for a rideshare or delivery platform** — channel count, because cabin and rear coverage are the reason that format exists.
- **You mainly want protection against a disputed claim** — GPS first, resolution second.
- **You want it invisible behind the mirror** — the single-lens bodies are the small ones; a tri-channel install is visible hardware with cable runs.
- **You swap the memory card rarely** — the card ceiling, which decides how many days of loop recording you hold before older footage is overwritten.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need GPS on a dash cam?**

Only if you expect the footage to be looked at by someone other than you. GPS stamps speed and location onto the clip, which is the data an insurer or police report can use; plain video establishes the sequence of events but not the speed of them. If you want the camera purely for your own record, it adds nothing.

**Will a dash cam void my car's warranty?**

No. A dash cam draws power from a 12V accessory outlet or a fuse tap, neither of which alters anything the manufacturer warrants. The genuine risk in a hardwire install is pulling from the wrong fuse, which is a wiring-safety question rather than a warranty one.

**How long does footage stay on the card before it loops?**

It scales with resolution, channel count and card size, which is why the ceilings differ: 256 GB on the single 1296P camera, 512 GB on the higher-resolution units. A single lens at 1296P holds days of driving on a large card; three channels with a 4K front lens fill the same card far faster. If you need footage more than a few days old, pull the clip off rather than buying a bigger card.
