Portable projectors look interchangeable in a product grid — palm-sized cylinders and boxes that all promise a movie-theater-sized picture from a backpack. What separates them is four published numbers: brightness, resolution, light source, and battery life (or the lack of a published one). This guide explains what each spec physically changes before you point one at a wall, then lays three models side by side so you can match the figures to your own setup.

## Brightness numbers aren't on the same scale

Brightness is published in lumens, but manufacturers don't all use the same measurement standard. Some list ANSI lumens, an industry-standardized method; others list ISO lumens, a different standard that tends to produce a higher number for similar real-world output. The two figures that differ most here — 200 ANSI lumens versus 450 ISO lumens — aren't simply "more than double as bright." They were measured differently, and the spec sheet won't flag that unless you check which standard sits next to the number.

What brightness actually changes is how dark the room needs to be and how big the image can get before it looks washed out. All three projectors here are built for a dim room or after dark; none is bright enough to compete with daylight or a lit living room.

## Native resolution sets the ceiling, not the typical picture

All three publish the same native resolution: 1920×1080, standard 1080p. None is working with more actual pixel detail than the others — the differences you'll see come from brightness, lens quality, and light source, not resolution.

Where resolution matters more than it looks is screen size. Stretch a 1080p image to a 150-inch wall and the same pixel count spreads thinner, so detail softens even though the resolution figure hasn't changed. At 60–80 inches, 1080p is normally plenty. Past 100 inches, the published screen-size range matters more than the resolution figure.

## Light source decides how it ages, not how sharp it looks

Two of the three use an LED light source; the third uses a laser. The difference shows up over years, not on day one. Both LED and laser light sources are rated for tens of thousands of hours and effectively outlast the rest of the projector, unlike older lamp-based models that needed bulb replacements. The practical difference between the two is less about longevity and more about color volume and brightness per watt, which is part of why the laser unit here publishes the highest lumen figure.

If you replace projectors every few years anyway, light source type matters less. If you want to buy once, laser is the one of these three built around not needing a replacement light engine.

## Battery life is a spec some projectors don't publish at all

This is the sharpest distinction in the table. Two of the three publish a runtime figure for an internal battery — one gives separate numbers for video and audio playback, the other a single eco-mode figure. The third doesn't publish a battery runtime at all; its product page describes pairing it with an external USB-C power bank instead of listing a built-in battery spec.

That's a real design difference, not a gap in the spec sheet. A projector with no published battery figure is telling you, by omission, that it expects a power source nearby. One with a published runtime is built to run unplugged for a defined stretch. Which one matters depends entirely on where you plan to use it.

## Matching the specs to your setup

- **You'll use it mostly near an outlet or a USB-C power bank** — battery life matters less; focus on brightness and screen-size range instead.
- **You want it off-grid for a backyard or camping movie night with no outlet in reach** — a published battery runtime is the spec to check first, and the model stating both video and audio hours gives the clearer picture.
- **You're projecting onto a wall larger than 100 inches** — weigh brightness and the published screen-size range together; a dim projector stretched that large looks washed out regardless of resolution.
- **You want the light engine to outlast a bulb-based projector** — check whether the light source is LED or laser; both skip traditional bulb replacement, but laser is built for the highest lumen output per watt here.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a higher ANSI or ISO lumen number always mean a brighter picture?**

Only within the same measurement standard. Comparing an ANSI figure to an ISO figure directly can be misleading, since the two standards don't measure brightness identically. When a spec sheet doesn't say which standard it used, treat the number as a rough guide, not an exact comparison point.

**Why doesn't one of these projectors publish a battery life figure?**

Because its product page describes it running from an external USB-C power bank rather than a built-in battery, so there's no internal runtime to publish. That's a design choice, not a missing data point — it trades a built-in battery for a lighter unit and the flexibility to use whatever power bank you already own.

**Is a laser light source worth it over LED for casual use?**

If you project a few times a month, LED is normally enough — both outlast the projector's realistic lifespan. Laser becomes more relevant if you want the highest brightness in a compact, battery-powered body, without checking lumens standards as closely.
