Rowing machines look like they come down to price and size, but three recognizable brands at three different price points use three different ways of generating resistance — and that single spec explains more about how the machine will feel than anything on the box. This guide walks through what resistance type, user weight rating, storage footprint and the monitor actually change, then lays three published spec sheets side by side so you can match them to your own space and body.

## Resistance type is what you'll feel, not just a number of levels

Magnetic resistance uses a flywheel and adjustable magnets to create a smooth, consistent drag at whatever level you set — pulling harder doesn't make the stroke heavier, because the resistance is fixed by the dial, not by your effort. That's what the budget Sunny unit and, in hybrid form, the Sole SR500 use. Air resistance, the mechanism in the Concept2 RowErg, works the opposite way: a fan-like flywheel inside a housing creates drag that scales with how hard you pull, so a harder stroke feels harder in real time. The "10-position damper" on an air rower doesn't set the resistance directly — it opens or closes an air intake that changes how much the flywheel catches the air, which changes how heavy the stroke feels at a given pace, not how much weight you're moving.

This is why "16 levels" and "10 positions" aren't comparable numbers across brands. A magnetic machine's level count is a true resistance scale; an air rower's damper setting is closer to a gear choice than a resistance choice.

## Max user weight is a frame-and-rail spec, not a strength spec

All three manufacturers publish a maximum user weight, and the three figures split cleanly by price tier: 250 lb, 450 lb, and 500 lb. This number isn't about how much resistance the machine can generate — it's an engineering limit on the seat rail, frame welds, and slide mechanism under repeated load. Exceeding it isn't a performance problem, it's a warranty and safety one. If you're shopping near a machine's limit, the published figure is the one number here you shouldn't round up on.

## Storage footprint decides whether it stays assembled

The Sunny unit publishes an exact folded size you can measure against a closet or under a bed before buying. The Concept2 splits into two pieces for storage with its own published dimensions, which is a different approach — not smaller, just divided. Sole's page describes a folding frame but, as of this writing, doesn't publish the folded footprint at all, only the assembled size and the shipping carton — which tells you nothing about where it lives day to day. An unpublished storage spec isn't necessarily a worse machine, but it is one you can't plan a room around from the spec sheet alone.

## The monitor is what keeps you rowing at the pace you think you are

A basic monitor (calories, stroke count, time, elapsed total) tells you what happened. A monitor with built-in programs, like the Sole's 11 presets plus a heart-rate-control mode, structures a workout for you if you don't want to plan one yourself. The Concept2's PM5 is built around a different job: it's the monitor the standardized indoor-rowing world calibrates against, which matters if you ever want your numbers to mean the same thing on a different Concept2 machine at a gym or in a class.

## Matching the specs to your space

- **Storage is tight** — the published folded footprint matters more than anything else; Sunny is the only one of the three that states it.
- **You're near the top of a weight range** — check the max user weight against your own, with room to spare, since it's a structural limit, not a soft guideline.
- **You want structured workouts without a coach** — the monitor's program count and modes matter more than the resistance mechanism.
- **You care about how the stroke feels, not just the workout afterward** — resistance type is the spec to research further, since magnetic and air rowers feel distinctly different at the same nominal effort.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is air resistance harder than magnetic resistance?**

Not inherently — they're different relationships between effort and drag. Magnetic resistance stays constant once you set the level; air resistance increases with how hard you pull. Someone who rows at a steady, moderate pace may find air resistance easier to settle into than a fixed magnetic level, and vice versa for someone who likes to vary intensity stroke to stroke.

**Why does one spec sheet not list a folded size?**

Manufacturers publish what they choose to publish, and not every brand measures a folded or stored footprint the same way — some only give the carton size it ships in. That gap shows up directly in the table below as "Not published" rather than a guess, which is the more useful signal if storage space is tight for you.

**Does a higher resistance-level count mean more resistance overall?**

Not by itself, and only within the same mechanism. More levels mean finer adjustment steps on a magnetic machine, not necessarily a higher ceiling — and the number isn't on the same scale as an air rower's damper position at all, as explained above.
